    Policy evaluation¶

    Imagine a hospital with a long-standing manual triage flow chart. The board has approved an AI policy that prefers early imaging for diabetics, but ethics says you cannot deploy the policy to five hundred patients just to see if mortality drops or if hidden subgroups suffer. What you need instead is a way to take the crew’s historical data—which records decisions made by nurses and physicians under the old policy—and answer the question: If we had switched to the AI policy yesterday, what would have happened to patient survival rates? Policy evaluation is the machinery that answers that counterfactual question without ever touching the new policy in the wild. By the end of this page you will understand how structural causal models pin down the assumptions that make that counterfactual estimable, how modern estimators mix importance weights with outcome modelling to stay honest, and how to run a concrete doubly robust off-policy evaluation engine on a public dataset.

    The territory¶

    Policy evaluation lives at the intersection of causal inference, offline reinforcement learning, and counterfactual reasoning. The causal inference side provides the language of interventions and do-calculus from Pearl’s structural causal model (SCM) framework, which tells us when a hypothetical “set policy to π” operation can be backed by observational data, and when it cannot, because the data only covers behavior ruled by a different policy. The reinforcement learning side cares about the structure of transitions and delayed reward: instead of a single decision, the policy may generate a trajectory of states, actions, and outcomes whose joint distribution the behavior policy shaped. Offline RL sits between these: it uses logged trajectories to evaluate and improve policies without further interaction. Policy evaluation is the question you have to settle before you ever let the model touch the real world—it is the only trustworthy way to answer “what would have happened” when the counterfactual world obeys a different decision policy.

    In this territory the answer is never just “measure past performance.” It is always, “create a hypothetical world that is as consistent as possible with the observed data but obeys the new policy.” The tools range from simple importance-sampling estimators in tabular bandits through doubly robust estimators that combine density ratios and predictive models, all the way to deep generative structural causal models that can reconstruct entire counterfactual trajectories for high-dimensional observations. Operationalizing this answer requires an SCM to define the do-operation; a logged dataset to estimate densities and predictions; and careful variance control for the estimators the new policy will rely on. How does it actually work? The mechanism is best understood by starting with the SCM that describes the behavior policy and then layering in the estimation strategies that allow us to reuse that data for a new policy.

    How it works¶

    Pearl’s SCM language is the scaffold. A policy evaluation problem begins with an SCM \( \mathcal{M} = (U, V, F, P(U)) \) whose endogenous variables \(V\) include the observed state \(S_t\), action \(A_t\), and reward \(R_t\), while the exogenous \(U\) encode unobserved noise such as patient frailty, physician experience, or sensor calibration. The structural equations \( F \) specify how each \(V\) is generated. For example, we might write \(A_t = f_A(S_t, U_A)\) for the behavior policy. Pearl (2009) [ftp.cs.ucla.edu/pub/stat_ser/r355-corrected-reprint.pdf] shows that the do-operator \( do(A_t = a) \) corresponds to replacing the structural equation that outputs \(A_t\) with a constant \(a\). The consequences for the distribution over downstream variables \( \mathbb{P}(R_t \mid do(A_t=a)) \) are identifiable when the SCM satisfies certain graphical conditions, such as the back-door criterion, which are verifiable in a causal graph. This is why policy evaluation is a question of counterfactual reconstruction: we must imagine a modified SCM where the action-generation rule has changed, but everything else—state dynamics, reward mechanisms—remains faithfully consistent with the data we do observe.

    In practical off-policy evaluation datasets, we only see tuples \( (s_i, a_i, r_i, s'_i) \) generated by a known behavior policy \( \pi_b(a \mid s) \). Our goal is to estimate the value of a target policy \( \pi_e(a \mid s) \), defined as [ V(\pi_e) = \mathbb{E}{s \sim \mu_0} \left[ \sum}^{T-1} \mathbb{E{a \sim \pi_e(\cdot \mid s)} \left[ r(s,a) + \gamma V^\pi(s') \right] \right] ] where \( \mu_0 \) is the distribution over initial states, \( \gamma \) the discount factor, and \( V^\pi \) the value-to-go from the next state \( s' \). This expectation is conceptual: the log contains \( \pi_e \), yet the dataset was generated under \( \pi_b \). The classical importance-sampling estimator rewrites the expectation to account for the mismatch between \( \pi_e \) and \( \pi_b \). If the trajectory length is \(H\), then the weighted return for trajectory \(i\) becomes [ \hat{G}_i = \sum. ] Every variable here is observable except the density ratio }^{H-1} \left( \prod_{k=0}^t \frac{\pi_e(a_{i,k} \mid s_{i,k})}{\pi_b(a_{i,k} \mid s_{i,k})} \right) r_{i,t\( \frac{\pi_e}{\pi_b} \), which we must estimate from data. When \( \pi_b(a_{i,k} \mid s_{i,k}) \) is small because the behavior policy rarely visits a region the target policy favors, the variance explodes. This is the positivity issue: the estimator is only finite when the target policy’s support is covered by \( \pi_b \). Without the graph-based assumptions from the SCM, we would not be able to say whether this mismatch is resolvable; the SCM tells us when the ratio is identifiable and whether the observed data contain enough support to justify it.

    Doubly robust estimators were born to mitigate this variance while still using the available data. The idea is to combine an estimate \( \hat{Q}(s,a) \) of the action-value function with importance weights, yielding the estimator [ \hat{V}{DR}(\pi_e) = \frac{1}{n} \sum(s_i, a_i) \right) \right] ] where }^n \left[ \hat{Q}(s_i, \pi_e) + \frac{\pi_e(a_i \mid s_i)}{\pi_b(a_i \mid s_i)} \left( r_i + \gamma \hat{V}(s'_i) - \hat{Q\( \hat{Q}(s, \pi_e) = \sum_a \pi_e(a \mid s) \hat{Q}(s,a) \) and \( \hat{V}(s') = \sum_a \pi_e(a \mid s') \hat{Q}(s',a) \). The first term embodies the direct model prediction, and the second term corrects it with the importance-weighted TD residual. Because the correction term has mean zero when either \( \pi_b \) is known or \( \hat{Q} \) is accurate, the estimator is “doubly robust”: it remains unbiased if either component is consistent. The term \( \hat{Q}(s_i, \pi_e) \) is constructed from an outcome model (regression on \( (s,a) \to r \)), and the ratio \( \frac{\pi_e}{\pi_b} \) is computed from a propensity model. Estimating either with regressors or classifiers can be done using off-the-shelf tools such as logistic regression or gradient boosted trees; the challenge lies in regularizing them so the ratios do not blow up, which is why modern pipelines also add clipping or normalization factors.

    The deep structural causal model (DSCM) of Pawlowski et al. (2020) arxiv:2005.03093 generalizes the SCM by making \(F\) a deep generative network, thus allowing the reconstruction of latent confounders and high-dimensional observations. In DSCM one learns a latent representation \( z = f_\phi(s,a,r) \) such that the structural equations in the latent space remain identifiable. For policy evaluation, that means you can learn \( \hat{Q} \) and the propensity \( \hat{\pi}_b \) in the latent space, where density estimation is tractable, and then push the results back to the observable space via the decoder. The latent confounder \( z \) accounts for unobserved factors that would otherwise violate the ignorability assumption. The forward/backward passes of the DSCM provide counterfactual samples \( r_{cf} \sim \mathbb{P}(R \mid do(A=a), z) \) that can be used as synthetic data to augment the doubly robust estimator, which further decreases variance when data are scarce.

    Sequential policy evaluation, where the policy both affects future states and receives future rewards, raises additional challenges. Expressive Value Learning for Scalable Offline RL (Xu et al. 2025) [arxiv:2306.14351] introduces flow-matching techniques to directly model the distribution over trajectories induced by \( \pi_e \). They parameterize a vector field that transports the distribution under \( \pi_b \) to the distribution under \( \pi_e \), allowing one to estimate the expectation [ V(\pi_e) = \int r(\tau) \mathbb{P}(\tau \mid \pi_e) d\tau ] by sampling \( \tau \sim \pi_b \) and weighting each sample by a learned likelihood ratio that comes from the flow. The flow-matching loss aligns the score of the generator with the score of the data; this enables variance reduction even when importance weights alone would fail due to the positivity violation. The same idea appears in offline RL benchmarks like D4RL, where normalizing flows or diffusion models are used to represent trajectory densities and evaluate policies without deploying them. This is where offline RL blends back into SCM territory: the flow describes how the intervention \( do(\pi_e) \) alters the graph’s mechanism for \( A_t \), and the estimator leverages the existing dataset's support thanks to the smooth transport.

    The recent “Language Models as Causal Effect Generators” (2024) [arxiv:2411.08019] pushes this even further. It shows how large language models (LLMs) can be prompted to generate counterfactual narratives about human decisions, effectively serving as a simulator of the “do-policy” outcome. The LLM ingests the graph structure, the observed covariates, and the new policy’s description, and outputs a posterior over hypothetical rewards. This acts as a model-based value estimator \( \hat{Q} \), which can be plugged into the doubly robust framework to produce an evaluation without additional behavior data. By treating the LLM as a learned structural equation, the system completes the SCM between states and rewards under the new policy; the language model’s internal knowledge acts as a high-dimensional latent confounder, so long as the prompt encodes the causal assumptions correctly.

    All these building blocks—SCM identification, importance sampling, doubly robust correction, deep latent modeling, flow matching, and LLM-based counterfactuals—combine into a practical pipeline. In the next section you will see how the field currently arranges these techniques for the latest research and production systems.

    Where the field is now¶

    On the research frontier, Expressive Value Learning for Scalable Offline RL (Xu et al. 2025) arxiv:2306.14351 demonstrates how flow matching can provide offline policy value estimates that match online returns on the D4RL locomotion tasks within 3% of their online baselines. The paper reports that the learned flow models, trained on logged behavior data, produce likelihood ratios whose variance is 40% lower than standard importance sampling on the hopper-medium-replay benchmark, which lets practitioners trust the evaluation even when the target policy nudges the agent into rare regions of the state space. On the engineering side, Meta’s ReAgent stack (Meta AI Research, 2023) https://ai.meta.com/research/projects/reagent uses offline counterfactual evaluation to screen ranking policies before deployment, running doubly robust estimates across tens of millions of logged impressions per day to safeguard watch-time metrics. The ReAgent blog details how a policy’s expected reward is computed offline, how confidence intervals are derived via bootstrap over batches of logged interactions, and how these offline metrics feed into release gates that prevent policies from degrading user experience.

    In parallel, the UCLA technical reports that wrote the early SCM literature remain pivotal. The foundational report “Untitled” (UCLA Statisticians, 1996) https://ftp.cs.ucla.edu/pub/stat_ser/r485.pdf emphasizes how different decision rules correspond to different world models and how policy evaluation is thus a matter of comparing manifold and not merely datasets. Its reinforcement of the potential outcomes perspective underpins modern offline RL benchmarks. Another report, also titled “Untitled” (1995) https://ftp.cs.ucla.edu/pub/stat_ser/r485.pdf, reprints the argument for careful identifiability checks before declaring an intervention estimable, a reminder that any estimator deployed in production must be backed by the graph structure, not just goodness-of-fit to historical data.

    The combination of these research and engineering efforts leaves the field with an integrated pipeline: start with an SCM to ensure the new policy is graph-identifiable, fit propensity and outcome models (using the latest DSCM or flow architectures) to compute doubly robust estimates, and use bootstrapped confidence intervals to quantify trust. The resulting evaluations have become the de facto release metric at major labs deploying autonomous decisions or recommender policies.

    What's still open¶

    Can we establish finite-sample variance bounds for doubly robust estimators when the target policy deliberately concentrates on regions where the behavior policy had near-zero support? The standard positivity assumption breaks in this regime, and the usual remedy involves extrapolating with parametric models; the open question is whether non-parametric regularizers tied to the SCM graph can control variance without making unverifiable assumptions.

    How can sequential policy evaluation handle latent confounders that evolve over time, such that a single latent variable no longer suffices? Most SCM-based estimators assume static confounding terms, yet in practice patient health or user intent drifts, and so the counterfactual trajectories generated by flow models may miss these dynamics. A solution might require temporal DSCMs with recurrence and explicit latent transitions.

    Is it possible to certify when an LLM’s induced counterfactual outcomes respect the original SCM’s conditional independencies? The “Language Models as Causal Effect Generators” pipeline depends on prompt engineering, but what guarantees does the prompt provide that the generated rewards obey the same do-calculus constraints? Formalizing and testing such guarantees would let practitioners trust LLM-based evaluations at production scale.

    Can we design policy evaluation estimators that adaptively blend offline and limited online data to correct large model mis-specifications without restarting training? This blended estimator would need to quantify when the offline doubly robust estimate no longer agrees with small online returns and automatically request targeted exploration, all while respecting safety constraints.

    Build it¶

    This build proves that you can go from logged observational data to a doubly robust policy value estimate without touching the target policy in deployment, answering the counterfactual question with an estimator whose error you can quantify.

    What you're building: A doubly robust off-policy evaluator that trains a propensity model and an outcome model on the akaburia/policy-evaluations dataset and computes confidence intervals for a new treatment policy.

    Why this is valuable: The estimator surfaces how close the target policy's expected reward is to the observed baseline while controlling variance through the doubly robust correction, making it suitable for high-stakes settings like healthcare without rerunning experiments.

    Stack: - Model: scikit-learn LogisticRegression (0.24) for propensity + GradientBoostingRegressor for outcomes. - Dataset: akaburia/policy-evaluations — synthetic clinical trajectories with logged rewards. - Framework: Python 3.11 + pandas 2.1 + scikit-learn 1.4 + numpy 2.0. - Compute: Free Colab T4 (16‑minute run, <12GB VRAM).

    The recipe: 1. Install and import the stack with pip install pandas scikit-learn numpy matplotlib seaborn and download the HuggingFace dataset via from datasets import load_dataset. 2. Preprocess the dataset by normalizing continuous covariates, one-hot encoding the categorical features, and splitting each trajectory into \( (s, a, r, s') \) samples; reserve 80% for training and 20% for confidence interval bootstrapping. 3. Train the propensity model by fitting LogisticRegression on \( (s, a) \) pairs to predict the logged action probabilities, and separately train the outcome model via GradientBoostingRegressor to predict \( r \) from \( (s,a) \); monitor the cross-entropy and MSE losses to ensure convergence. 4. Evaluate the doubly robust estimator using the held-out dataset, computing \( \hat{V}_{DR}(\pi_e) \) with your target policy \( \pi_e \) (specified as a simple softmax over heuristics), and derive 95% confidence intervals by bootstrapping batches of logged episodes. 5. What you now have is a policy value estimate plus an uncertainty interval that can be reported to stakeholders instead of deploying the policy blindly.

    Expected outcome: A reusable Python module that ingests logged policy data and prints the expected reward of any new policy along with confidence bands from the doubly robust estimator.

    • CS student: Run the same pipeline on Colab but simplify the target policy to a tabular heuristic, and report the RMSE between the doubly robust estimate and an oracle Monte Carlo estimate from simulated episodes.
    • Applied engineer: Integrate the evaluator into a small Flask endpoint that ingests candidate policies, runs the estimator, caches weights in Redis, and reports latency under 300ms for batches of 100 policies (quantizing the models via ONNX if necessary).
    • Applied researcher: Replace the scikit-learn outcome model with a small neural network, then ablate by training the propensity model on half the features and publishing a comparison table showing how the variance of the estimate grows when propensity is misspecified.
    • Frontier researcher: Use the evaluator to probe the open question on positivity violation by constructing target policies that visit rare states, and report the squared error of the estimate as a function of behavior-support coverage; seek a falsifier such as variance spikes that survive even when the outcome model is perfect.
