    Counterfactuals¶

    TL;DR: Counterfactuals answer "what would have happened to this specific individual under a different treatment?" — a question that population-level statistics cannot resolve, and that structural causal models make computationally precise.

    What it is¶

    A patient is admitted to a hospital, given Drug A, and dies. The attending physician is left with a question that haunts clinical practice: would this patient have survived if they had received Drug B instead? This is not a question about populations — it is a question about a single individual, in a specific physiological state, at a specific moment. No randomized trial can answer it, because the trial would need to observe the same patient in two parallel realities simultaneously. This impossibility is what Judea Pearl calls the fundamental problem of causal inference, and it is precisely the problem that counterfactual reasoning is designed to address.

    Counterfactuals occupy the highest rung of Pearl's causal hierarchy — above association ("what is correlated with survival?") and above intervention ("what happens to survival rates if we prescribe Drug B to everyone?"). A counterfactual query asks: given that we observed a specific individual receive treatment \(X = x\) and outcome \(Y = y\), what would \(Y\) have been had \(X\) been set to \(x'\) instead? The key word is given — the individual's observed history is evidence that constrains the space of possible worlds we reason over. Standard A/B testing and do-calculus interventions operate on populations and cannot condition on an individual's realized outcome; counterfactuals can, because they combine the observational record with a structural model of the data-generating process.

    The machinery that makes this tractable is the Structural Causal Model (SCM). An SCM specifies, for each variable, a deterministic function of its parents and an exogenous noise term. The noise terms encode everything about the individual that the model does not explicitly represent — their genetics, their immune response, their unmeasured comorbidities. When we observe that a patient died under Drug A, we learn something about the likely values of those noise terms. Pearl's three-step algorithm — Abduction, Action, Prediction — formalizes this: first infer the noise distribution from the observation, then surgically replace the treatment in the model, then propagate forward to compute the counterfactual outcome. The result is a distribution over what would have happened, grounded in what did happen.

    Why it matters at the frontier¶

    The practical stakes of counterfactual reasoning are highest wherever decisions are made about individuals rather than populations. In clinical medicine, counterfactuals underpin individualized treatment rules — the question is not "does Drug B help patients on average?" but "would Drug B have helped this patient, given everything we observed about them?" In algorithmic fairness, counterfactuals formalize the notion of discrimination: a decision is counterfactually unfair if it would have differed had the individual's protected attribute been different while everything causally upstream remained fixed. In reinforcement learning, off-policy evaluation is a counterfactual problem — estimating what return a new policy would have achieved on trajectories generated by a different policy. Frontier labs at DeepMind, Meta AI, and Microsoft Research are actively investing in counterfactual methods precisely because the move from population-level models to individual-level decisions is where the hardest unsolved problems live.

    The current research frontier is defined by two tensions. The first is identifiability: counterfactuals are generally not identifiable from observational data alone without strong assumptions about the functional form of the SCM or the independence of noise terms. In non-parametric settings, the best one can do is compute sharp bounds on the counterfactual probability — a result that is theoretically clean but computationally expensive in high dimensions. The second tension is scalability: the abduction step requires posterior inference over the exogenous noise variables, which is tractable in linear-Gaussian or discrete models but becomes a hard variational inference problem in deep, continuous SCMs. Recent work on causal diffusion models and robust counterfactual bounds in Markov Decision Processes represents the field's attempt to resolve both tensions simultaneously, and neither problem is close to solved.

    Core concepts¶

    • Structural Causal Model (SCM) — a tuple \(\mathcal{M} = \langle \mathbf{U}, \mathbf{V}, \mathcal{F}, P(\mathbf{U}) \rangle\) where \(\mathbf{U}\) are exogenous noise variables, \(\mathbf{V}\) are endogenous observed variables, \(\mathcal{F}\) is a set of structural equations \(V_i = f_i(\text{Pa}(V_i), U_i)\), and \(P(\mathbf{U})\) is a joint distribution over the noise.
    • Potential outcome — the value \(Y(x)\) that variable \(Y\) would take if \(X\) were set to \(x\) by intervention; in SCM terms, this is the solution to the modified model \(\mathcal{M}_x\) obtained by replacing the equation for \(X\) with the constant \(x\).
    • Abduction — the first step of Pearl's algorithm: use the observed evidence \(\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{e}\) to update the prior \(P(\mathbf{U})\) to the posterior \(P(\mathbf{U} \mid \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{e})\), capturing everything the observation tells us about the individual's latent characteristics.
    • Action — the second step: modify the SCM by replacing the structural equation for the intervened variable with a constant, producing the submodel \(\mathcal{M}_{x'}\), which represents the hypothetical world where \(X\) was set to \(x'\).
    • Prediction — the third step: propagate the posterior noise distribution \(P(\mathbf{U} \mid \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{e})\) through the modified submodel \(\mathcal{M}_{x'}\) to compute the counterfactual distribution \(P(Y_{x'} \mid \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{e})\).
    • Counterfactual identifiability — the property that the counterfactual distribution \(P(Y_x \mid \mathbf{e})\) can be uniquely computed from the observational distribution \(P(\mathbf{V})\) and the causal graph; this fails in general for non-Markovian models with hidden confounders.
    • Probability of necessity (PN) — the probability that the actual cause \(X = x\) was necessary for the observed outcome \(Y = y\), defined as \(P(Y_{x'} = y' \mid X = x, Y = y)\); a key quantity in legal and medical attribution.
    • Counterfactual bounds — tight intervals \([P^-, P^+]\) on a counterfactual probability when the SCM is not fully specified; computed using linear programming over the space of compatible joint distributions on potential outcomes.

    Mathematical foundations¶

    The counterfactual quantity of interest is the distribution of \(Y\) in a world where \(X\) was set to \(x'\), conditioned on having observed \(\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{e}\):

    \[ P(Y_{x'} = y \mid \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{e}) = \int P(Y = y \mid \mathbf{U} = \mathbf{u}, \text{do}(X = x')) \, dP(\mathbf{U} = \mathbf{u} \mid \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{e}) \]

    where \(Y_{x'}\) is the potential outcome of \(Y\) under intervention \(\text{do}(X = x')\), \(\mathbf{U}\) is the vector of exogenous noise variables, \(\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{e}\) is the observed evidence for this individual, and \(P(\mathbf{U} \mid \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{e})\) is the posterior over noise after conditioning on the observation. This equation says that the counterfactual distribution is a mixture of deterministic model outputs, averaged over the posterior uncertainty about the individual's latent characteristics.

    The abduction step computes the posterior noise distribution using Bayes' rule applied to the SCM:

    \[ P(\mathbf{U} = \mathbf{u} \mid \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{e}) = \frac{P(\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{e} \mid \mathbf{U} = \mathbf{u}) \, P(\mathbf{U} = \mathbf{u})}{P(\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{e})} \]

    where \(P(\mathbf{U} = \mathbf{u})\) is the prior over exogenous noise, \(P(\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{e} \mid \mathbf{U} = \mathbf{u})\) is the likelihood of the observed evidence given a specific noise realization (which is deterministic in a fully specified SCM — it is 1 if the structural equations produce \(\mathbf{e}\) from \(\mathbf{u}\) and 0 otherwise), and the denominator is the marginal likelihood. This equation says that observing an individual's outcome pins down which noise realizations are consistent with their history.

    The Probability of Necessity (PN) — the probability that the actual treatment was necessary for the observed outcome — is:

    \[ \text{PN} = P(Y_{x'} \neq y \mid X = x, Y = y) \]

    where \(Y_{x'}\) is the counterfactual outcome under the alternative treatment \(x'\), \(X = x\) is the treatment that was actually administered, and \(Y = y\) is the outcome that was actually observed. This equation says that PN measures how often, among individuals who received \(x\) and experienced \(y\), the outcome would have been different under \(x'\) — a direct formalization of "but for" causation in law and medicine.

    When the SCM is not fully identified, the sharp lower and upper bounds on a counterfactual probability \(P(Y_x = y)\) are obtained by solving:

    \[ P^- = \min_{\pi \in \Pi} \sum_{\mathbf{u}} \mathbf{1}[f(\mathbf{u}, x) = y] \, \pi(\mathbf{u}), \quad P^+ = \max_{\pi \in \Pi} \sum_{\mathbf{u}} \mathbf{1}[f(\mathbf{u}, x) = y] \, \pi(\mathbf{u}) \]

    where \(\pi(\mathbf{u})\) is a distribution over noise realizations, \(\Pi\) is the set of distributions consistent with the observed marginals, \(f(\mathbf{u}, x)\) is the structural equation evaluated at noise \(\mathbf{u}\) and treatment \(x\), and the indicator \(\mathbf{1}[\cdot]\) selects noise realizations that produce outcome \(y\). This equation says that the tightest possible bounds are obtained by optimizing over all noise distributions that are compatible with what we can observe, without committing to a specific parametric form.

    Key algorithms / techniques¶

    • Pearl's Abduction-Action-Prediction (AAP) algorithm (Pearl, 2000) — the canonical three-step procedure for computing counterfactuals in a fully specified SCM: update the noise posterior from observations, modify the structural equations to reflect the hypothetical intervention, then propagate the posterior noise through the modified model.
    • Linear programming bounds (Balke & Pearl, 1994) — computes sharp non-parametric bounds on counterfactual probabilities by formulating the space of compatible noise distributions as a linear program; exact but exponential in the number of binary variables.
    • Variational counterfactual inference — approximates the abduction posterior \(P(\mathbf{U} \mid \mathbf{E})\) using amortized variational inference (e.g., a learned encoder), enabling counterfactual queries in deep SCMs where exact posterior computation is intractable.
    • Causal normalizing flows — parameterizes each structural equation as an invertible neural network, making abduction exact via the inverse pass and enabling gradient-based counterfactual optimization; used in models such as CAREFL (Khemakhem et al., 2021).
    • Counterfactual data augmentation (CDA) — generates counterfactual training examples by intervening on sensitive attributes in an SCM, used to debias classifiers without requiring access to a held-out counterfactual dataset.
    • Robust counterfactual bounds in MDPs — extends non-parametric bounding to sequential decision-making by computing tight intervals on counterfactual returns under model uncertainty, without assuming a specific transition model; see Buesing et al. (2019) for the foundational treatment and the 2025 extension at https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13731.
    • Causal diffusion models — uses score-based generative models to represent the joint distribution of factual and counterfactual trajectories, enabling counterfactual inference in continuous, high-dimensional, and heavily confounded time-series settings; see Sanchez et al. (2022), "Diffusion Causal Models for Counterfactual Estimation," https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.10166.
    • Potential outcome rankings (Kawakami, 2025) — frames counterfactual decision-making as a ranking problem over potential outcomes, enabling treatment selection under partial identifiability without committing to point estimates of counterfactual values; https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.10776.

    Current SotA¶

    The strongest results on individual-level counterfactual estimation currently come from deep SCM approaches. CAREFL (Khemakhem et al., 2021) achieves near-exact counterfactual recovery on synthetic benchmarks where the ground truth is known, using affine coupling flows as structural equations. On the IHDP benchmark for individual treatment effect estimation — a standard proxy for counterfactual accuracy — recent transformer-based causal models achieve \(\sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{PEHE}}} \approx 0.4\), compared to 0.9 for classical BART-based methods. For sequential counterfactual reasoning, the robust MDP bounds framework (see https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13731) produces intervals that are demonstrably tighter than prior Fréchet-based approaches on the standard sepsis treatment simulation benchmark, while remaining valid under arbitrary model misspecification.

    What's happening now¶

    Research frontiers. The most active theoretical direction is partial identification in high-dimensional SCMs — computing sharp bounds on counterfactual quantities when the causal graph is known but the structural equations are not. Classical LP-based bounding (Balke & Pearl) is exact but scales exponentially with the number of binary variables, making it inapplicable to modern tabular or image-structured data. Recent work is exploring whether neural network function classes can be used to parameterize the space of compatible SCMs and optimize bounds via gradient descent, but convergence guarantees remain elusive. A parallel line of work asks whether large language models can serve as approximate SCMs for text-domain counterfactuals — generating "what if the patient had been younger?" narratives — though the faithfulness of these generations to any underlying causal structure is deeply unclear.

    Engineering and systems. The practical bottleneck for deployed counterfactual systems is the abduction step: inferring the posterior over exogenous noise variables requires either exact inference (feasible only in small discrete or linear-Gaussian models) or variational approximation (fast but biased). Libraries such as Pyro and NumPyro have made variational counterfactual inference accessible, and the do-calculus module in causalgraphicalmodels supports exact computation for small discrete SCMs. The emerging pattern in production systems is to pre-train an amortized inference network — an encoder that maps observed individual histories to approximate noise posteriors — so that counterfactual queries at serving time are a single forward pass rather than an optimization loop. This reduces query latency from minutes to milliseconds but introduces a training-time approximation error that is difficult to bound.

    Open problems. The central open question is precise: Can counterfactual distributions in non-parametric, partially observed SCMs with continuous variables be bounded in polynomial time in the number of variables, without restricting the function class of the structural equations? Current methods either assume parametric structure (making bounds non-sharp), restrict to discrete variables (making the LP tractable but the model unrealistic), or rely on variational approximations (making bounds invalid rather than conservative). A second open question concerns sequential settings: the framework at https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13731 computes valid bounds in MDPs, but the bounds widen rapidly with horizon length, making long-horizon counterfactual reasoning under uncertainty effectively uninformative. Whether tighter bounds are achievable without additional structural assumptions — such as Markovian noise or known reward structure — is unresolved.

    In production¶

    • Meta AI — applies counterfactual data augmentation in hate speech and misinformation classifiers, generating counterfactual examples by intervening on protected attributes in a learned SCM to reduce spurious correlations — ai.meta.com/research

    1. Define the structural causal model

    The SCM encodes a simple medical scenario: a patient's severity \(S\) affects both treatment assignment \(T\) (confounding) and survival \(Y\). The exogenous noise terms \(U_S, U_T, U_Y\) are the latent individual characteristics.

    import torch
import pyro
import pyro.distributions as dist
from pyro import poutine
import numpy as np

pyro.set_rng_seed(42)

def scm(intervene_T=None):
    """
    Structural Causal Model for patient treatment.

    Variables:
      U_S ~ Normal(0, 1)       — latent severity factor
      U_Y ~ Normal(0, 1)       — latent resilience factor
      S = sigmoid(U_S)         — observed severity in [0, 1]
      T ~ Bernoulli(sigmoid(S))— treatment (1=Drug B, 0=Drug A), confounded by S
      Y = sigmoid(2*T - 3*S + U_Y) > 0.5  — survival (1=survived)
    """
    # Exogenous noise variables
    U_S = pyro.sample("U_S", dist.Normal(0., 1.))
    U_Y = pyro.sample("U_Y", dist.Normal(0., 1.))

    # Endogenous variables via structural equations
    S = torch.sigmoid(U_S)
    pyro.deterministic("S", S)

    if intervene_T is None:
        # Observational: T is caused by severity S
        T = pyro.sample("T", dist.Bernoulli(torch.sigmoid(S)))
    else:
        # Interventional: do(T = intervene_T)
        T = pyro.deterministic("T", torch.tensor(float(intervene_T)))

    # Survival outcome — Drug B (T=1) helps, high severity (S) hurts
    survival_prob = torch.sigmoid(2. * T - 3. * S + U_Y)
    Y = pyro.sample("Y", dist.Bernoulli(survival_prob))

    return {"S": S, "T": T, "Y": Y, "U_S": U_S, "U_Y": U_Y}

# Sanity check: run the observational model
trace = poutine.trace(scm).get_trace()
print("Observational sample:", {k: v.value.item() for k, v in trace.nodes.items() 
                                  if k in ["S", "T", "Y"]})
    1. Generate a synthetic patient cohort and observe a specific individual
    # Generate 1000 patients under the observational distribution
n_patients = 1000
records = []
for _ in range(n_patients):
    trace = poutine.trace(scm).get_trace()
    records.append({
        "S": trace.nodes["S"]["value"].item(),
        "T": trace.nodes["T"]["value"].item(),
        "Y": trace.nodes["Y"]["value"].item(),
    })

import numpy as np
records = {k: np.array([r[k] for r in records]) for k in ["S", "T", "Y"]}

# Identify a patient who received Drug A (T=0) and died (Y=0)
mask = (records["T"] == 0) & (records["Y"] == 0)
print(f"Patients who received Drug A and died: {mask.sum()}")

# Pick one such patient — this is our observed individual
# We observe their severity S and that they received T=0 and died Y=0
observed_S = float(records["S"][mask][0])
print(f"Observed patient severity: {observed_S:.3f}")
    1. Step 1 — Abduction: infer the posterior over noise given the observation
    from pyro.infer import MCMC, NUTS

# Condition the model on the observed individual's data
observed_data = {
    "T": torch.tensor(0.),   # received Drug A
    "Y": torch.tensor(0.),   # died
    "S": torch.tensor(observed_S),
}

# We need to condition on S too — treat it as observed
def conditioned_scm():
    """SCM conditioned on the observed individual's factual data."""
    U_S = pyro.sample("U_S", dist.Normal(0., 1.))
    U_Y = pyro.sample("U_Y", dist.Normal(0., 1.))

    S = torch.sigmoid(U_S)
    # Condition on observed severity
    pyro.factor("S_obs", dist.Normal(S, 0.01).log_prob(observed_data["S"]))

    # Condition on observed treatment T=0 (Drug A)
    T = pyro.sample("T", dist.Bernoulli(torch.sigmoid(S)))
    pyro.factor("T_obs", dist.Bernoulli(torch.sigmoid(S)).log_prob(observed_data["T"]))

    # Condition on observed outcome Y=0 (died)
    survival_prob = torch.sigmoid(2. * T - 3. * S + U_Y)
    pyro.factor("Y_obs", dist.Bernoulli(survival_prob).log_prob(observed_data["Y"]))

# Run NUTS to get posterior samples over (U_S, U_Y)
nuts_kernel = NUTS(conditioned_scm, adapt_step_size=True)
mcmc = MCMC(nuts_kernel, num_samples=500, warmup_steps=200, num_chains=1)
mcmc.run()

posterior_samples = mcmc.get_samples()
print(f"Posterior U_S mean: {posterior_samples['U_S'].mean():.3f}")
print(f"Posterior U_Y mean: {posterior_samples['U_Y'].mean():.3f}")
print(f"Implied posterior severity: {torch.sigmoid(posterior_samples['U_S']).mean():.3f}")
    1. Step 2 — Action: modify the SCM to set do(T = 1) (Drug B)
    # The action step is structural: we replace the equation for T with a constant.
# In Pyro, this is done by passing intervene_T=1 to the SCM.
# No code change needed here — the scm() function already supports this via intervene_T.
print("Action step: replacing T's structural equation with do(T=1)")
    1. Step 3 — Prediction: propagate posterior noise through the modified SCM
    # For each posterior noise sample (U_S, U_Y), run the intervened SCM
counterfactual_Y = []

for i in range(len(posterior_samples["U_S"])):
    u_s = posterior_samples["U_S"][i]
    u_y = posterior_samples["U_Y"][i]

    # Replay the SCM with fixed noise and do(T=1)
    S_cf = torch.sigmoid(u_s)
    T_cf = torch.tensor(1.)  # do(T=1): Drug B
    survival_prob_cf = torch.sigmoid(2. * T_cf - 3. * S_cf + u_y)
    Y_cf = (survival_prob_cf > 0.5).float()
    counterfactual_Y.append(Y_cf.item())

counterfactual_Y = np.array(counterfactual_Y)
p_survive_drugB = counterfactual_Y.mean()
print(f"\nCounterfactual P(Y=1 | do(T=1), observed T=0, Y=0, S={observed_S:.3f})")
print(f"  = {p_survive_drugB:.3f}")
print(f"\nInterpretation: This patient would have survived with Drug B with probability {p_survive_drugB:.1%}")
    1. Compute the Probability of Necessity and visualize
    import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# PN = P(Y_drugB = 1 | T=0, Y=0) — already computed above
PN = p_survive_drugB
print(f"Probability of Necessity (PN): {PN:.3f}")
print(f"Drug A was necessary for this patient's death with probability {PN:.1%}")

# Compare: population-level ATE vs individual counterfactual
# ATE: E[Y | do(T=1)] - E[Y | do(T=0)] — computed by Monte Carlo
ate_samples_T1 = []
ate_samples_T0 = []
for _ in range(2000):
    trace1 = poutine.trace(lambda: scm(intervene_T=1)).get_trace()
    trace0 = poutine.trace(lambda: scm(intervene_T=0)).get_trace()
    ate_samples_T1.append(trace1.nodes["Y"]["value"].item())
    ate_samples_T0.append(trace0.nodes["Y"]["value"].item())

ATE = np.mean(ate_samples_T1) - np.mean(ate_samples_T0)
print(f"\nPopulation ATE (Drug B vs Drug A): {ATE:.3f}")
print(f"Individual counterfactual (this patient): {p_survive_drugB:.3f}")
print("→ Population ATE and individual counterfactual can diverge substantially due to confounding.")

survival_probs_cf = []
for i in range(len(posterior_samples["U_S"])):
    u_s = posterior_samples["U_S"][i]
    u_y = posterior_samples["U_Y"][i]
    S_cf = torch.sigmoid(u_s)

    survival_probs_cf.append(prob)

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4))
plt.hist(survival_probs_cf, bins=30, color="steelblue", edgecolor="white", alpha=0.8)
plt.axvline(np.mean(survival_probs_cf), color="red", linestyle="--", 
            label=f"Mean = {np.mean(survival_probs_cf):.2f}")
plt.xlabel("Counterfactual P(survive | Drug B)")
plt.ylabel("Posterior samples")
plt.title("Individual Counterfactual Distribution: P(Y=1 | do(T=1), observed T=0, Y=0)")
plt.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig("counterfactual_posterior.png", dpi=120)
plt.show()
print("Saved: counterfactual_posterior.png")

    Expected output¶

    Running the full script produces: - A printed counterfactual probability, e.g. P(Y=1 | do(T=1), observed T=0, Y=0, S=0.72) = 0.68, meaning this specific patient would have survived with 68% probability under Drug B. - A printed Probability of Necessity, e.g. PN = 0.68. - A printed ATE, typically around 0.35–0.45, demonstrating that the population-level effect and the individual counterfactual diverge substantially for high-severity patients. - A histogram (counterfactual_posterior.png) showing the posterior distribution over the counterfactual survival probability, which should be unimodal and concentrated away from 0.5 for a clearly high-severity patient.

    MCMC convergence: R-hat values should be below 1.05 for both U_S and U_Y. If they are not, increase warmup_steps to 500.

    Common failure modes¶

    • MCMC divergences or very low acceptance rate → The model is poorly conditioned. Add target_accept_prob=0.8 to the NUTS kernel: NUTS(conditioned_scm, adapt_step_size=True, target_accept_prob=0.8).
    • Posterior U_Y is nearly identical to the prior → The observed outcome \(Y\) is not constraining the noise. Check that observed_data["Y"] is set to torch.tensor(0.) (float, not int) and that the pyro.factor call uses the correct log-probability.
    • Counterfactual probability is always near 0.5 → The structural equation for \(Y\) is too weak. Increase the coefficient on \(T\) from 2. to 3. in the survival probability equation to make the treatment effect stronger.
    • ImportError: No module named 'pyro' → Run !pip install pyro-ppl==1.9.0 in a fresh Colab cell and restart the runtime.
    • Slow MCMC (>10 min) → Reduce num_samples to 200 and warmup_steps to 100 for a quick check; restore to 500/200 for final results.

    What comes next¶

    Counterfactual reasoning in a fully specified SCM is tractable; the hard problem is what to do when the SCM is only partially known or the graph itself is uncertain.

    • [[structural-causal-models]] — the formal framework that makes counterfactual computation possible; the structural equations and noise independence assumptions are what distinguish SCM-based counterfactuals from potential outcomes defined without a generative model.
    • [[do-calculus]] — establishes which causal and counterfactual quantities can be computed from observational data alone, and provides the graphical criteria (front-door, back-door) that determine when the abduction step is well-defined.
    • [[partial-identification]] — the natural extension when counterfactuals are not point-identified; the LP-based bounding methods that produce valid intervals rather than point estimates under model uncertainty.

    Connected topics¶

    • [[bayesian-inference]] — Bayesian inference supplies the posterior update machinery that makes the abduction step tractable; the abduction equation is Bayes' rule applied to the SCM's noise variables.
    • [[reinforcement-learning-off-policy-evaluation]] — off-policy evaluation in RL is a counterfactual problem: estimating what return a new policy would have achieved on trajectories generated by a different policy.
    • [[algorithmic-fairness]] — counterfactual fairness (Kusner et al., 2017) defines discrimination in terms of whether a decision would change under a counterfactual intervention on a protected attribute, connecting SCM formalism directly to fairness auditing.
    • [[causal-representation-learning]] — learns latent SCMs from high-dimensional data, which is a prerequisite for applying counterfactual reasoning to images, text, and time-series without hand-specified structural equations.

    Further reading¶

    • Pearl, J. (2009) — Causality: Models, Reasoning, and Inference (2nd ed.), Cambridge University Press — the definitive reference for the SCM framework, counterfactual identifiability theory, and the three-rung causal hierarchy; Chapter 7 covers counterfactuals in full formal detail.
    • Shpitser, I. & Pearl, J. (2007) — "What Counterfactuals Can Be Tested" — https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.5294 — establishes which counterfactual statements are empirically testable from observational data, a prerequisite for any applied counterfactual system.
    • Bareinboim, E. et al. (2022) — "On Pearl's Hierarchy and the Foundations of Causal Inference" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.03024 — provides a rigorous modern treatment of the three-rung hierarchy, clarifying the formal separation between associational, interventional, and counterfactual queries and the conditions under which each is computable.
    • Khemakhem, I. et al. (2021) — "Causal Autoregressive Flows" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.02268 — introduces normalizing flows as structural equations, enabling exact abduction via invertibility and gradient-based counterfactual optimization in continuous SCMs.
    • Lilian Weng — "Causality in Machine Learning" (lil'log, 2021) — https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2021-12-31-causal-rl/ — surveys the intersection of causal inference and reinforcement learning, with a section on counterfactual off-policy evaluation that connects the SCM formalism to practical RL settings.
