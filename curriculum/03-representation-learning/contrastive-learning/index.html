    Contrastive Learning¶

    TL;DR: Contrastive learning trains encoders by pulling semantically similar pairs together and pushing dissimilar pairs apart in a shared latent space — enabling powerful, label-free representations that transfer to downstream tasks with little or no fine-tuning.

    What it is¶

    Imagine teaching someone what a "guitar" is — not by handing them a dictionary definition, but by showing them an acoustic guitar and an electric guitar side by side ("these are the same kind of thing") while contrasting both against a violin ("this is different"). No labels required. The learner builds an internal model of "guitar-ness" purely from the structure of comparisons. This is the core intuition behind contrastive learning: rather than predicting absolute categories, a model learns by mapping relative semantic distances. Things that belong together should land close in the model's internal space; things that don't belong together should land far apart.

    The practical consequence of this framing is enormous. Supervised learning requires a human to assign a label to every training example — a bottleneck that limits scale. Contrastive learning sidesteps this by constructing its own supervision signal from the data itself. Two crops of the same image, a sentence and its translation, a photo and its caption — these are "positive pairs" that the model should map to nearby points. Everything else in the batch is a "negative" the model should push away. This self-supervised signal can be generated automatically at internet scale, which is why contrastive methods have become the dominant pre-training strategy for vision, language, and multimodal systems.

    What makes contrastive learning work in practice is the geometry it imposes on the learned space. A well-trained contrastive encoder doesn't just cluster positives together — it distributes representations uniformly across the hypersphere, preventing the model from collapsing to a trivial solution where everything maps to the same point. This balance between alignment (positives close) and uniformity (representations spread out) is the central tension the field has spent the last five years learning to control.

    Why it matters at the frontier¶

    Contrastive learning is the mechanism that made large-scale multimodal foundation models possible. CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) demonstrated that training a vision encoder and a text encoder jointly with a contrastive objective on 400 million web-scraped image-caption pairs produces visual representations that transfer to dozens of downstream tasks without any task-specific fine-tuning. That result reframed the entire question of visual pre-training: the bottleneck was never model capacity, it was the supervision signal. Contrastive learning at scale provides a supervision signal that is effectively unlimited, which is why every major vision-language model — from ALIGN to SigLIP to the vision towers inside LLaVA — uses some variant of this approach.

    The open problems that remain are not incremental. Contrastive learning still struggles with dimensional collapse — the tendency for representations to concentrate in a low-dimensional subspace of the embedding space, wasting capacity and degrading transfer. It also struggles with hard negatives: when a batch contains two images of different guitars, the model is penalized for treating them as dissimilar, even though they are semantically close. And as systems scale beyond two modalities (image + text + audio + depth + video), the pairwise cosine similarity that underlies InfoNCE becomes geometrically inadequate — a problem that recent work on Gramian-based alignment (2025) is beginning to address. These are the questions that determine whether contrastive representations can serve as the universal substrate for multimodal AI.

    Core concepts¶

    • Positive pair — two views of the same underlying semantic content (e.g., two augmented crops of one image, or an image and its caption) that the model is trained to map to nearby points in embedding space.
    • Negative pair — two examples from different semantic categories that the model is trained to push apart; in practice, all other examples in the batch serve as negatives.
    • Embedding space / latent space — the high-dimensional vector space (typically \(\mathbb{R}^{128}\) to \(\mathbb{R}^{1024}\)) where encoders map inputs; contrastive training shapes the geometry of this space.
    • InfoNCE loss — the standard contrastive objective, derived from noise-contrastive estimation, that trains the model to identify the true positive among \(N-1\) negatives in a batch.
    • Temperature parameter \(\tau\) — a scalar that controls the sharpness of the similarity distribution; low \(\tau\) makes the model focus on hard negatives, high \(\tau\) smooths the gradient signal.
    • Alignment — the property that positive pairs have high cosine similarity; a necessary condition for useful representations but not sufficient on its own.
    • Uniformity — the property that representations are spread uniformly across the unit hypersphere; prevents dimensional collapse and ensures the embedding space is used efficiently.
    • Dimensional collapse — the failure mode where representations span only a low-dimensional subspace despite the encoder having high capacity; a central unsolved problem in contrastive learning.

    Mathematical foundations¶

    The core contrastive objective is the InfoNCE loss. For a batch of \(N\) image-text pairs, the loss for a single image \(i\) is:

    \[ \mathcal{L}_i = -\log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(z_i^I, z_i^T) / \tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp(\text{sim}(z_i^I, z_j^T) / \tau)} \]

    where \(z_i^I \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is the \(\ell_2\)-normalized image embedding for example \(i\), \(z_i^T \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is the \(\ell_2\)-normalized text embedding for the same example, \(z_j^T\) ranges over all \(N\) text embeddings in the batch (including the true positive at \(j = i\)), \(\text{sim}(\cdot, \cdot)\) is cosine similarity, and \(\tau > 0\) is the temperature hyperparameter. This equation says that the model is solving an \(N\)-way classification problem: given image \(i\), identify which of the \(N\) text embeddings is its true match. The loss is minimized when the true positive scores higher than all negatives.

    The full symmetric CLIP loss averages the image-to-text and text-to-image directions:

    \[ \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( \mathcal{L}_i^{I \to T} + \mathcal{L}_i^{T \to I} \right) \]

    where \(\mathcal{L}_i^{I \to T}\) is the per-example loss for identifying the correct text given image \(i\) — computed as the InfoNCE expression above — and \(\mathcal{L}_i^{T \to I}\) is the symmetric counterpart: the loss for identifying the correct image given text \(i\), computed by transposing the similarity matrix and applying the same softmax-cross-entropy. The factor \(\frac{1}{2N}\) averages over both directions and all examples in the batch. This symmetry ensures that both encoders receive gradient signal from both directions simultaneously.

    Wang and Isola (2020) decomposed the contrastive objective into two interpretable terms. The alignment loss is:

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} = \mathbb{E}_{(x, x^+) \sim p_{\text{pos}}} \left[ \| f(x) - f(x^+) \|^2 \right] \]

    where \(f(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is the \(\ell_2\)-normalized embedding of input \(x\), \(x^+\) is a positive view drawn from the positive pair distribution \(p_{\text{pos}}\), and \(\|\cdot\|^2\) is squared Euclidean distance on the unit hypersphere. This term says that the model should minimize the distance between positive pairs — it measures how well the encoder aligns semantically equivalent inputs.

    The uniformity loss is:

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{uniform}} = \log \mathbb{E}_{x, y \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} p_{\text{data}}} \left[ e^{-2\|f(x) - f(y)\|^2} \right] \]

    where \(x\) and \(y\) are two independently sampled data points from the marginal data distribution \(p_{\text{data}}\), and \(f(\cdot)\) is again the \(\ell_2\)-normalized encoder. This term is minimized when representations are maximally spread across the unit hypersphere — it penalizes representations that cluster together regardless of semantics. The tension between these two terms is the fundamental trade-off contrastive learning must navigate: perfect alignment would collapse all positives to a single point, while perfect uniformity would push all points apart regardless of semantics.

    Key algorithms / techniques¶

    • SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020) — trains a single vision encoder using two random augmentations of each image as positives; introduced the projection head (a small MLP applied before the loss) and showed that large batch sizes (4096+) are critical for performance.
    • MoCo / MoCo v2 / MoCo v3 (He et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2020, 2021) — uses a momentum encoder (an exponential moving average of the online encoder) and a memory queue to decouple batch size from the number of negatives, enabling contrastive training on single-GPU hardware.
    • CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) — trains a vision encoder and a text encoder jointly with symmetric InfoNCE on 400M web-scraped image-caption pairs; the resulting embeddings enable zero-shot classification by comparing image embeddings to text embeddings of class names.
    • ALIGN (Jia et al., 2021) — scales CLIP-style training to 1.8 billion noisy image-text pairs without aggressive data cleaning, showing that scale compensates for noise.
    • BYOL (Grill et al., 2020) — eliminates explicit negatives entirely by using an online/target network asymmetry with a stop-gradient operation; demonstrated that uniformity can be maintained without negative pairs, challenging the assumption that negatives are necessary.
    • SimSiam (Chen & He, 2021) — further simplified BYOL by removing the momentum encoder, showing that stop-gradient alone prevents collapse; provided theoretical grounding for why asymmetric architectures avoid trivial solutions.
    • SigLIP (Zhai et al., 2023) — replaces the softmax-based InfoNCE with a sigmoid loss that treats each pair independently, removing the dependency on large batch sizes and enabling more stable training at scale.
    • GRAM (2025) — extends contrastive alignment beyond pairwise cosine similarity to \(k > 2\) modalities using Gramian volume as the alignment metric, enabling geometrically principled multi-modal pre-training.

    Current SotA¶

    OpenCLIP's largest ViT-G/14 model trained on LAION-2B achieves 80.1% zero-shot top-1 accuracy on ImageNet (Cherti et al., 2022). SigLIP-So400m achieves 83.2% zero-shot ImageNet accuracy while training stably at smaller batch sizes than softmax CLIP (Zhai et al., 2023). On cross-modal retrieval, the EVA-CLIP-18B model (Sun et al., 2023) achieves 83.8% zero-shot ImageNet top-1 and sets state-of-the-art on Flickr30k image-text retrieval at R@1 = 97.4%, representing the current ceiling for pure contrastive pre-training before instruction tuning is applied.

    What's happening now¶

    Research frontiers. The most active theoretical question is dimensional collapse: contrastive encoders routinely use only a small fraction of their nominal embedding dimension, concentrating representations in a low-dimensional subspace. This wastes capacity and degrades transfer to tasks that require fine-grained distinctions. Recent work has approached this through spectral regularization (penalizing the condition number of the embedding covariance matrix) and through feature decorrelation objectives borrowed from Barlow Twins (Zbontar et al., 2021). The deeper question — how to mathematically guarantee full-rank representations without relying on massive batch sizes or heuristic negative mining — remains open and is the subject of active work at DeepMind, Meta AI, and several academic groups. A precise formulation: can we derive a closed-form condition on the encoder architecture and loss that is both necessary and sufficient to prevent dimensional collapse for arbitrary data distributions?

    Engineering and systems. The engineering challenge in contrastive learning at scale is the all-pairs similarity matrix. For a batch of \(N\) examples, computing the \(N \times N\) similarity matrix requires \(O(N^2)\) memory, which becomes prohibitive at \(N > 32{,}768\). SigLIP's per-pair sigmoid loss partially addresses this by enabling chunked computation, but the fundamental quadratic scaling remains. Distributed contrastive training across hundreds of GPUs requires careful all-gather operations to share embeddings across devices — a pattern implemented in OpenCLIP's gather_features utility and in Google's JAX-based training infrastructure. The practical consequence is that state-of-the-art contrastive training still requires TPU pods or multi-node GPU clusters, and reducing this compute requirement without sacrificing representation quality is an active engineering priority.

    Open problems. Three specific questions define the frontier. First: how do we construct hard negatives automatically and at scale without introducing false negatives (two semantically similar examples incorrectly treated as dissimilar)? Current approaches use nearest-neighbor mining or curriculum strategies, but none have a principled solution to the false-negative problem. Second: can contrastive objectives be extended to more than two modalities without the combinatorial explosion of pairwise losses? The GRAM framework (2025) proposes Gramian volume as a \(k\)-way alignment metric, but its scaling properties and gradient behavior at large \(k\) are not yet well understood. Third: what is the right inductive bias for the projection head — the MLP applied before the contrastive loss — and why does removing it after training improve downstream performance? This gap between the representation that minimizes the contrastive loss and the representation that transfers best remains theoretically unexplained.

    In production¶

    • Google — uses CLIP-style contrastive pre-training as the vision backbone for Google Lens, processing over 12 billion visual searches per month; the SigLIP architecture (Zhai et al., 2023) is the current production vision encoder — https://research.google/blog/
    • Meta AI — deploys contrastive image-text embeddings in Instagram's visual search and content recommendation systems, operating at billions of daily active users; the ImageBind model (Girdhar et al., 2023) extends contrastive alignment to six modalities in production — https://ai.meta.com/research/
    • OpenAI — CLIP embeddings power the image understanding components of GPT-4V and the DALL-E 3 safety classifiers; the original ViT-L/14 CLIP model processes millions of images daily in content moderation pipelines — https://openai.com/research/
    • Stability AI — uses CLIP text encoders as the conditioning signal in Stable Diffusion; the text encoder's contrastive pre-training is the mechanism by which text prompts are mapped to the latent space that guides image generation — https://stability.ai/research
    • LinkedIn — uses dual-encoder contrastive models for job-candidate matching and skills embedding, with the embedding space trained on implicit positive pairs derived from user engagement signals at hundreds of millions of members — https://engineering.linkedin.com/

    Minimum Valuable Build¶

    What you're building: A dual-encoder CLIP-style model trained on Flickr8k that maps images and captions into a shared 256-dimensional embedding space using InfoNCE loss.

    Why this build: This demonstrates the complete contrastive learning loop — positive pair construction, symmetric InfoNCE computation, and the emergence of a joint embedding space where you can retrieve images from text queries and vice versa.

    Stack: Python 3.10, PyTorch 2.1, torchvision 0.16, transformers 4.38 (HuggingFace), openai/clip-vit-base-patch32 as the frozen text encoder backbone, Flickr8k dataset (~1GB, free download).

    Estimated time: 2–3 hours on a free Colab T4 GPU (16GB VRAM). Runs comfortably within the free tier.

from datasets import load_dataset

    1. Define the dual-encoder architecture
    import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F
from torchvision import models, transforms
from transformers import CLIPTokenizer, CLIPTextModel

device = torch.device("cuda" if torch.cuda.is_available() else "cpu")
print(f"Using device: {device}")

class ImageEncoder(nn.Module):
    """ResNet-50 backbone with a linear projection head."""
    def __init__(self, embed_dim=256):
        super().__init__()
        backbone = models.resnet50(weights=models.ResNet50_Weights.IMAGENET1K_V1)
        self.encoder = nn.Sequential(*list(backbone.children())[:-1])  # remove final FC
        self.projection = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(2048, 512),
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(512, embed_dim)
        )

    def forward(self, x):
        features = self.encoder(x).squeeze(-1).squeeze(-1)  # (B, 2048)
        return F.normalize(self.projection(features), dim=-1)  # (B, embed_dim)


class TextEncoder(nn.Module):
    """Frozen CLIP text encoder with a linear projection head."""
    def __init__(self, embed_dim=256):
        super().__init__()
        self.tokenizer = CLIPTokenizer.from_pretrained("openai/clip-vit-base-patch32")
        self.encoder = CLIPTextModel.from_pretrained("openai/clip-vit-base-patch32")
        # Freeze the CLIP text encoder — we only train the projection
        for param in self.encoder.parameters():
            param.requires_grad = False
        self.projection = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(512, 512),
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(512, embed_dim)
        )

    def forward(self, captions):
        tokens = self.tokenizer(
            captions, padding=True, truncation=True,
            max_length=77, return_tensors="pt"
        ).to(device)
        with torch.no_grad():
            text_features = self.encoder(**tokens).pooler_output  # (B, 512)
        return F.normalize(self.projection(text_features), dim=-1)  # (B, embed_dim)


image_encoder = ImageEncoder(embed_dim=256).to(device)
text_encoder = TextEncoder(embed_dim=256).to(device)

# Sanity check: verify output shapes
dummy_img = torch.randn(4, 3, 224, 224).to(device)
dummy_txt = ["a dog on a beach", "a cat on a sofa", "a bird in a tree", "a car on a road"]
img_emb = image_encoder(dummy_img)
txt_emb = text_encoder(dummy_txt)
assert img_emb.shape == (4, 256), f"Expected (4, 256), got {img_emb.shape}"
assert txt_emb.shape == (4, 256), f"Expected (4, 256), got {txt_emb.shape}"
print("Shape check passed:", img_emb.shape, txt_emb.shape)
    1. Implement the symmetric InfoNCE loss
    class InfoNCELoss(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, temperature=0.07):
        super().__init__()
        self.temperature = temperature

    def forward(self, image_embeddings, text_embeddings):
        """
        image_embeddings: (B, D) — L2-normalized image vectors
        text_embeddings:  (B, D) — L2-normalized text vectors
        Returns: scalar loss (symmetric InfoNCE)
        """
        B = image_embeddings.shape[0]
        # Compute (B, B) similarity matrix
        logits = torch.matmul(image_embeddings, text_embeddings.T) / self.temperature
        # Ground truth: diagonal entries are the true positives
        labels = torch.arange(B, device=device)
        # Symmetric loss: image→text and text→image
        loss_i2t = F.cross_entropy(logits, labels)
        loss_t2i = F.cross_entropy(logits.T, labels)
        return (loss_i2t + loss_t2i) / 2.0

criterion = InfoNCELoss(temperature=0.07)

# Sanity check: loss on random embeddings should be close to log(B)
import math
dummy_loss = criterion(img_emb, txt_emb)
print(f"Random-init loss: {dummy_loss.item():.3f} (expected ~{math.log(4):.3f})")
    1. Build the DataLoader
    from torch.utils.data import Dataset, DataLoader
from PIL import Image
import io

image_transform = transforms.Compose([
    transforms.Resize((224, 224)),
    transforms.ToTensor(),
    transforms.Normalize(mean=[0.485, 0.456, 0.406], std=[0.229, 0.224, 0.225])
])

class Flickr8kDataset(Dataset):
    def __init__(self, hf_dataset, transform):
        self.data = hf_dataset
        self.transform = transform

    def __len__(self):
        return len(self.data)

    def __getitem__(self, idx):
        item = self.data[idx]
        image = item["image"].convert("RGB")
        # Flickr8k has a list of captions; take the first one
        caption = item["caption"][0] if isinstance(item["caption"], list) else item["caption"]
        return self.transform(image), caption

flickr_dataset = Flickr8kDataset(dataset, image_transform)
dataloader = DataLoader(flickr_dataset, batch_size=64, shuffle=True,
                        num_workers=2, pin_memory=True)
print(f"Batches per epoch: {len(dataloader)}")
    1. Train for 10 epochs
    optimizer = torch.optim.AdamW(
    list(image_encoder.parameters()) + list(text_encoder.projection.parameters()),
    lr=3e-4, weight_decay=1e-4
)
scheduler = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(optimizer, T_max=10)

for epoch in range(10):
    image_encoder.train()
    text_encoder.train()
    total_loss = 0.0

    for batch_idx, (images, captions) in enumerate(dataloader):
        images = images.to(device)
        img_emb = image_encoder(images)
        txt_emb = text_encoder(captions)

        loss = criterion(img_emb, txt_emb)
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        # Gradient clipping prevents instability at low temperature
        torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(
            list(image_encoder.parameters()) + list(text_encoder.projection.parameters()),
            max_norm=1.0
        )
        optimizer.step()
        total_loss += loss.item()

    scheduler.step()
    avg_loss = total_loss / len(dataloader)
    print(f"Epoch {epoch+1:02d} | Loss: {avg_loss:.4f} | LR: {scheduler.get_last_lr()[0]:.2e}")
    1. Evaluate with text-to-image retrieval (Recall@1)
    image_encoder.eval()
text_encoder.eval()

all_img_embs = []
all_txt_embs = []

with torch.no_grad():
    for images, captions in dataloader:
        images = images.to(device)
        all_img_embs.append(image_encoder(images))
        all_txt_embs.append(text_encoder(captions))

all_img_embs = torch.cat(all_img_embs, dim=0)  # (N, 256)
all_txt_embs = torch.cat(all_txt_embs, dim=0)  # (N, 256)

# Text-to-image retrieval: for each text, find the closest image
similarity = torch.matmul(all_txt_embs, all_img_embs.T)  # (N, N)
top1_preds = similarity.argmax(dim=1)  # (N,)
ground_truth = torch.arange(len(all_img_embs), device=device)
recall_at_1 = (top1_preds == ground_truth).float().mean().item()
print(f"Text→Image Recall@1: {recall_at_1 * 100:.1f}%")
# A random baseline would be ~0.01% for 8k examples
# After 10 epochs you should see 15–30% Recall@1 on this small dataset
    1. Save the checkpoint
    torch.save({
    "image_encoder": image_encoder.state_dict(),
    "text_projection": text_encoder.projection.state_dict(),
    "embed_dim": 256,
    "temperature": 0.07,
}, "flickr8k_clip_checkpoint.pt")
print("Checkpoint saved: flickr8k_clip_checkpoint.pt")

    Expected output¶

    After 10 epochs on a Colab T4 (~45 minutes), you should see: - Training loss dropping from ~4.15 (random init, ≈ log(64)) to ~2.8–3.2 - Text→Image Recall@1 of 15–30% on the full Flickr8k test split (random baseline: ~0.01%) - A saved checkpoint flickr8k_clip_checkpoint.pt (~100MB) containing the image encoder and text projection weights

    The embedding space can be visualized with UMAP: images of dogs, cats, and outdoor scenes should form loose clusters, with their corresponding captions landing nearby.

    Common failure modes¶

    • Loss stays at log(batch_size) and doesn't decrease → The image encoder's gradients are not flowing; check that image_encoder.parameters() is included in the optimizer and that requires_grad=True for the ResNet backbone layers.
    • CUDA out of memory on T4 → Reduce batch size from 64 to 32; the loss will be noisier but training will converge.
    • Recall@1 stays below 5% after 10 epochs → Temperature is too low (try 0.1–0.2); very low temperatures make gradients vanish for all but the hardest negatives, which is counterproductive on small datasets.
    • KeyError: 'caption' when loading Flickr8k → The HuggingFace dataset field name may vary by split; inspect dataset[0].keys() and adjust the field name in Flickr8kDataset.__getitem__.
    • Text encoder projection not training → Verify that text_encoder.projection.parameters() (not text_encoder.parameters()) is passed to the optimizer; the CLIP backbone is intentionally frozen.

    • OpenCLIP (LAION-AI) — the reference open-source implementation of CLIP training, including SigLIP, CoCa, and scaling experiments: https://github.com/mlfoundations/open_clip
    • CLIP (OpenAI) — the original CLIP model weights and inference code, with ViT-B/32 through ViT-L/14@336px: https://github.com/openai/CLIP
    • SimCLR (Google Research) — the original SimCLR implementation in TensorFlow with the full augmentation pipeline: https://github.com/google-research/simclr
    • MoCo v3 (Facebook Research) — the MoCo v3 implementation with ViT backbone support: https://github.com/facebookresearch/moco-v3
    • HuggingFace CLIP model hub — pre-trained CLIP variants including openai/clip-vit-base-patch32, openai/clip-vit-large-patch14, and laion/CLIP-ViT-H-14-laion2B-s32B-b79K: https://huggingface.co/models?other=clip

    What comes next¶

    Understanding contrastive learning's geometry makes the design choices in self-supervised vision transformers precise rather than heuristic — the alignment/uniformity trade-off directly explains why DINO's centering operation prevents collapse.

    • [[self-supervised-learning]] — contrastive learning is one family within self-supervised learning; the broader landscape includes masked autoencoders and predictive methods that don't use explicit negatives.
    • [[multimodal-representation-learning]] — CLIP-style contrastive pre-training is the foundation of multimodal systems; joint embedding spaces are used for retrieval, generation, and grounding.
    • [[disentangled-representations]] — contrastive objectives shape the global geometry of embedding spaces but don't enforce factorization of semantic factors; disentanglement methods address what contrastive learning leaves unstructured.

    Further reading¶

    • Radford et al. (2021) — "Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020 — the CLIP paper; essential for understanding how contrastive objectives scale to web data and enable zero-shot transfer.
    • Oord et al. (2018) — "Representation Learning with Contrastive Predictive Coding" — https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748 — derives the InfoNCE bound from first principles and establishes the connection between contrastive objectives and mutual information maximization.
    • Wang & Isola (2020) — "Understanding Contrastive Representation Learning through Alignment and Uniformity on the Hypersphere" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.10242 — provides the alignment/uniformity decomposition that gives geometric meaning to what InfoNCE is actually optimizing.
    • Lilian Weng (2021) — "Contrastive Representation Learning" — https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2021-05-31-contrastive/ — a comprehensive survey covering SimCLR, MoCo, BYOL, and CLIP with unified notation; useful for seeing how the methods relate to each other.
    • Cherti et al. (2022) — "Reproducible Scaling Laws for Contrastive Language-Image Learning" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.07143 — empirically derives the scaling laws for CLIP-style training, showing the precise relationship between compute budget, dataset size, and downstream zero-shot performance.
    • Zbontar et al. (2021) — "Barlow Twins: Self-Supervised Learning via Redundancy Reduction" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03230 — introduces a cross-correlation-based objective that directly targets dimensional collapse, offering a complementary perspective on why uniformity matters and how to enforce it without negatives.
