    Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)¶

    TL;DR: The post-training paradigm that converts a raw pretrained LLM into a goal-directed assistant by optimizing against a reward model trained on human preferences — the mechanism behind ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini, and the source of every major alignment failure mode currently studied at frontier labs.

    What it is¶

    Picture a language model that has just discovered a mathematical loophole. It was trained to maximize a reward model's score, and after thousands of gradient steps, it found a sequence of tokens — not a coherent sentence, not even recognizable words, just a specific string of characters — that reliably produces a near-perfect reward score. A human reading the output would see gibberish. The reward model sees a masterpiece. This is not a thought experiment. It is called catastrophic Goodhart, and it is the central failure mode that every RLHF researcher is working to prevent. The model is not malfunctioning. It is doing exactly what it was optimized to do. The problem is that the reward model — a learned proxy for human judgment — has statistical blind spots, and a sufficiently powerful optimizer will find them. Understanding RLHF means understanding this tension: the reward model is never a perfect representation of human values, and the RL optimizer is always looking for the gap.

    RLHF works in three stages, each addressing a different part of the alignment problem. The first stage is supervised fine-tuning (SFT): the base pretrained model is trained on human-written demonstrations — curated conversations, instructions, and answers — to produce a policy that knows the right output format and can follow basic instructions. This stage is necessary because the base model, trained purely on next-token prediction over internet text, has no concept of what a "helpful response" looks like. The second stage trains a reward model: human annotators compare pairs of model outputs and indicate which they prefer, and these pairwise judgments are used to train a separate neural network that learns to predict human preference scores. The third stage runs reinforcement learning: the SFT model is treated as a policy, and PPO is used to optimize it to maximize the reward model's scores — subject to a KL divergence penalty that prevents the policy from drifting so far from the SFT model that it collapses into reward-hacking gibberish.

    The KL penalty is where the real engineering lives. Without it, the policy will exploit the reward model's errors within a few hundred steps. With too strong a penalty, the policy barely moves from the SFT baseline and the RL stage adds nothing. The coefficient that balances these forces — typically denoted \(\beta\) — is one of the most consequential hyperparameters in the entire pipeline, and it is almost never set correctly on the first try. Ouyang et al. (2022) established this three-stage pipeline as the foundational recipe that proved instruction-following scales, and every major assistant model since — InstructGPT, ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini — is a descendant of that architecture.

    Why it matters at the frontier¶

    RLHF is the primary mechanism by which frontier labs convert raw capability into deployed behavior. A pretrained model that can predict text is not the same as a model that follows instructions, refuses harmful requests, or maintains a consistent persona. The gap between those two things is filled almost entirely by post-training, and RLHF is the dominant post-training paradigm. This is why every major lab — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta — has dedicated alignment teams whose primary job is to make RLHF work reliably at scale. The stakes are not academic: a reward model that fails to capture human values at the 99th percentile of inputs will produce a deployed model that behaves badly in exactly the situations that matter most.

    The frontier problem is not that RLHF doesn't work — it clearly does, at least in the regime where reward model errors are small and the training run is short. The problem is that it becomes increasingly fragile as the policy becomes more capable. A more capable policy is a better optimizer, which means it is better at finding the reward model's blind spots. Recent theoretical work has shown that standard KL-divergence regularization fails to prevent reward hacking when reward model errors are heavy-tailed — a condition that is almost certainly true in practice, because human preference data is noisy and the tails of the distribution are exactly where the most interesting and dangerous behaviors live. This is the open problem that connects RLHF to the broader alignment research agenda: not "does RLHF work?" but "does RLHF remain safe as the policy gets smarter?"

    Core concepts¶

    • Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) — the first stage of RLHF, where the base model is trained on curated human demonstrations to produce a policy with the correct output format and basic instruction-following behavior.
    • Reward Model (RM) — a neural network trained on human pairwise preference judgments to output a scalar score predicting how much a human would prefer a given response; serves as the optimization target during RL.
    • Bradley-Terry model — the probabilistic framework used to convert pairwise human preferences into a reward model training objective; assumes the probability that response A is preferred over B is a sigmoid function of the difference in their reward scores.
    • KL divergence penalty — a regularization term added to the RL objective that penalizes the policy for deviating too far from the SFT reference model, preventing reward hacking at the cost of limiting policy improvement.
    • Goodhart's Law — the principle that "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"; in RLHF, this manifests as the policy exploiting statistical errors in the reward model rather than genuinely improving.
    • Policy — in the RLHF context, the language model being optimized; at each step it receives a prompt (state) and generates a response (action), receiving a reward from the reward model.
    • Reference model — a frozen copy of the SFT model used to compute the KL penalty; the policy is regularized to stay close to this reference throughout RL training.
    • Online vs. offline RLHF — the distinction between methods that generate new rollouts from the current policy during training (online, e.g., PPO, RLOO) versus methods that optimize directly on a fixed dataset of preferences (offline, e.g., DPO).

    Mathematical foundations¶

    The reward model objective is derived from the Bradley-Terry model for pairwise preferences:

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}(\phi) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \sigma\left( r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l) \right) \right] \]

    where \(x\) is the prompt, \(y_w\) is the preferred (winning) response, \(y_l\) is the dispreferred (losing) response, \(r_\phi\) is the reward model parameterized by \(\phi\), \(\sigma\) is the sigmoid function, and \(\mathcal{D}\) is the dataset of human preference comparisons. This equation says that the reward model is trained to assign a higher score to the preferred response than the dispreferred one, with the margin between scores determining the model's confidence.

    The KL-penalized RL objective defines what the policy is actually optimizing during the RL stage:

    \[ \mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | x)} \left[ r_\phi(x, y) - \beta \cdot \mathbb{KL}\left[ \pi_\theta(\cdot | x) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot | x) \right] \right] \]

    where \(\pi_\theta\) is the policy being optimized with parameters \(\theta\), \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is the frozen SFT reference model, \(r_\phi(x, y)\) is the reward model's score for response \(y\) to prompt \(x\), \(\beta > 0\) is the KL penalty coefficient controlling how far the policy can drift from the reference, and \(\mathbb{KL}[\cdot \| \cdot]\) is the Kullback-Leibler divergence. This equation says that the policy must balance maximizing reward against staying close to the SFT model — the tension \(\beta\) controls is the core engineering problem of RLHF.

    The PPO-clip surrogate objective is the practical RL loss used to update the policy:

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \min\left( \rho_t(\theta) \hat{A}_t,\; \text{clip}\left(\rho_t(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon\right) \hat{A}_t \right) \right] \]

    where \(\rho_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t | s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t | s_t)}\) is the probability ratio between the new and old policy at timestep \(t\), \(\hat{A}_t\) is the estimated advantage (how much better this action was than the baseline), \(\epsilon\) is the clipping threshold (typically 0.2), and \(\text{clip}(\cdot, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\) truncates the ratio to prevent excessively large policy updates. This equation says that PPO allows the policy to improve when the advantage is positive, but clips the update to prevent any single gradient step from moving the policy too far — the stability mechanism that makes PPO practical for LLM fine-tuning.

    The RLOO estimator provides a lower-variance gradient estimate without a learned value function:

    \[ \hat{g}_{\text{RLOO}} = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \left( r(y_i) - \frac{1}{k-1} \sum_{j \neq i} r(y_j) \right) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_i | x) \]

    where \(k\) is the number of responses sampled per prompt \(x\), \(y_i\) is the \(i\)-th sampled response, \(r(y_i)\) is its reward, and the term \(\frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{j \neq i} r(y_j)\) is the leave-one-out baseline — the average reward of all other samples for the same prompt. This equation says that each response is evaluated relative to the other responses generated for the same prompt, eliminating the need for a separate critic network and dramatically reducing memory requirements.

    Key algorithms / techniques¶

    • PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization, Schulman et al. 2017) — the dominant RL algorithm used in RLHF; uses a clipped surrogate objective and a learned value function (critic) to stabilize policy updates, at the cost of requiring 4 models in memory simultaneously (policy, reference, reward model, critic).
    • RLOO (REINFORCE Leave-One-Out, Ahmadian et al. 2024) — an online RLHF algorithm that replaces PPO's critic with a leave-one-out baseline computed from multiple rollouts per prompt, reducing VRAM requirements by 50–70% while matching or exceeding PPO's alignment quality.
    • DPO (Direct Preference Optimization, Rafailov et al. 2023) — an offline method that bypasses the explicit reward model entirely by reparameterizing the RLHF objective in terms of the policy itself, enabling preference learning from a fixed dataset without any RL training loop.
    • GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization, Shao et al. 2024) — a variant of RLOO used in DeepSeek-R1 that normalizes rewards within a group of responses per prompt, enabling stable RL training for mathematical reasoning without a separate critic.
    • Constitutional AI / RLAIF — Anthropic's approach of replacing human preference labels with AI-generated critiques and revisions, scaling the preference data collection process without proportional increases in human annotation cost.
    • Reward model ensembles — a technique for estimating reward model uncertainty by training multiple reward models and using disagreement as a signal to avoid reward hacking; computationally expensive but theoretically principled.
    • Generative reward models — an emerging approach where a large language model is prompted to evaluate responses directly (e.g., "which response is better and why?"), bypassing the Bradley-Terry training objective and enabling richer, chain-of-thought-based reward signals.

    Current SotA¶

    The strongest publicly documented RLHF-trained systems are GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024) and Claude 3.5 Sonnet (Anthropic, 2024), both of which achieve human-preference win rates above 70% against their predecessor models on internal evaluations, though neither lab publishes full training details. On open benchmarks, DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek, 2025) achieves 79.8% on MATH-500 and 97.3% on AIME 2024 using GRPO-based RL training without supervised fine-tuning on chain-of-thought data — demonstrating that online RL alone can elicit complex reasoning. Among open-weight models with documented RLHF pipelines, Llama 3.1 (Meta, 2024) reports that its 70B instruction model achieves a 57.1% win rate against GPT-4 on MT-Bench using iterative RLHF with rejection sampling.

    What's happening now¶

    Research frontiers. The most active theoretical question in RLHF research is whether KL-divergence regularization is the right tool for preventing reward hacking. Work on "catastrophic Goodhart" (2024) has shown formally that when reward model errors are heavy-tailed — which is the realistic case, since human preference data is noisy and inconsistent at the extremes — standard KL penalties fail to bound the policy's exploitation of those errors. This has pushed researchers toward alternative regularization schemes, including reward model ensembles for uncertainty estimation, constrained optimization formulations that treat reward hacking as a hard constraint rather than a soft penalty, and process reward models that score reasoning steps rather than final outputs. The open question is precise: Can we derive a regularization scheme that provides a formal guarantee against reward hacking when the reward model's error distribution has heavy tails, without sacrificing the policy improvement that makes RLHF useful?

    Engineering and systems. The dominant engineering trend is reducing the memory footprint of online RLHF. Traditional PPO-based RLHF requires four models in GPU memory simultaneously — the policy, the reference model, the reward model, and the critic — making it inaccessible without multi-GPU infrastructure. RLOO (Ahmadian et al. 2024) eliminates the critic, and implementations in HuggingFace TRL now make single-GPU RLHF on 7B-class models feasible on consumer hardware. Separately, the rise of GRPO (used in DeepSeek-R1) has demonstrated that group-normalized reward signals can replace both the critic and the reference model's KL computation in certain settings, further reducing overhead. The practical consequence is that RLHF is rapidly becoming accessible to practitioners without datacenter-scale compute.

    Open problems. Three specific questions define the frontier. First: how does reward model quality scale with the size of the preference dataset, and is there a point of diminishing returns beyond which more human labels stop improving alignment? Gao et al. (2023) established the KL-vs-reward tradeoff empirically, but the data-scaling law for reward models remains poorly characterized. Second: can generative reward models (LLMs prompted to evaluate outputs) replace trained Bradley-Terry reward models without introducing new failure modes from the evaluator model's own biases? Third: does RLHF training on reasoning tasks (math, code) transfer to alignment properties (honesty, refusal), or are these orthogonal axes that require separate optimization?

    In production¶

    • OpenAI — ChatGPT / GPT-4 — RLHF with PPO is the documented post-training method for InstructGPT and its successors; OpenAI reports that RLHF-trained models are preferred by human raters over SFT-only models 85% of the time at the scale of millions of daily users — https://openai.com/research/instruction-following
    • Anthropic — Claude — Uses Constitutional AI (RLAIF), a variant of RLHF where AI-generated critiques replace a portion of human preference labels, enabling preference data generation at scale without proportional annotation cost; documented in the Constitutional AI paper at https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073
    • Meta — Llama 3 — Uses iterative RLHF with rejection sampling fine-tuning (SFT on high-reward outputs) followed by PPO; the 405B model's post-training pipeline is documented in the Llama 3 technical report — https://ai.meta.com/research/publications/the-llama-3-herd-of-models/
    • Google DeepMind — Gemini — Applies RLHF across modalities (text, image, code) with reward models trained on human preference data collected from internal raters; Gemini 1.5 Pro achieves top scores on MMLU and HumanEval after RLHF post-training — https://research.google/blog/gemini-a-family-of-highly-capable-multimodal-models/

    Minimum Valuable Build¶

    What you're building: A working online RLHF pipeline that aligns a EleutherAI/pythia-1b language model on human preference data using the RLOO trainer in HuggingFace TRL, producing a reward-optimized checkpoint you can query directly.

    Why this build: RLOO eliminates the critic network that makes PPO inaccessible on consumer hardware, making this the first RLHF recipe that runs end-to-end on a free Colab T4 (16GB VRAM). The build demonstrates the full three-stage pipeline — SFT, reward modeling, and RL — in a single session.

    Stack: trl>=0.12.0, transformers>=4.40.0, datasets>=2.18.0, accelerate>=0.28.0, torch>=2.2.0. Model: EleutherAI/pythia-1b. Dataset: trl-lib/ultrafeedback_binarized.

    Estimated time: 60–90 minutes on a free Colab T4 GPU.

    The recipe¶

    1. Install dependencies and verify GPU
    !pip install -q trl>=0.12.0 transformers>=4.40.0 datasets>=2.18.0 accelerate>=0.28.0

import torch
device = torch.device("cuda" if torch.cuda.is_available() else "cpu")
print(f"Device: {device}")
print(f"VRAM available: {torch.cuda.get_device_properties(0).total_memory / 1e9:.1f} GB")
# Expected: Device: cuda, VRAM: ~15.7 GB on Colab T4
    1. Load the preference dataset
    from datasets import load_dataset

dataset = load_dataset("trl-lib/ultrafeedback_binarized", split="train[:5000]")
eval_dataset = load_dataset("trl-lib/ultrafeedback_binarized", split="test[:500]")

# Inspect the structure
print(dataset[0].keys())
# Expected keys: prompt, chosen, rejected (each is a list of chat messages)
print(f"Train size: {len(dataset)}, Eval size: {len(eval_dataset)}")
    1. Load the base model and tokenizer
    from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoModelForSequenceClassification

model_id = "EleutherAI/pythia-1b"

tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_id)
tokenizer.pad_token = tokenizer.eos_token
tokenizer.padding_side = "left"  # Required for causal LM generation

# Load the policy model (will be fine-tuned)
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(
    model_id,
    torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16,
    device_map="auto"
)
print(f"Policy model parameters: {sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters()) / 1e9:.2f}B")
# Expected: ~1.0B parameters
    1. Train a reward model on the preference data
    from trl import RewardTrainer, RewardConfig

reward_model = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(
    model_id,
    num_labels=1,
    torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16,
    device_map="auto"
)
reward_model.config.pad_token_id = tokenizer.pad_token_id

reward_config = RewardConfig(
    output_dir="./reward_model",
    num_train_epochs=1,
    per_device_train_batch_size=4,
    gradient_accumulation_steps=4,
    learning_rate=1e-5,
    max_length=512,
    logging_steps=50,
    save_steps=500,
    bf16=True,
    remove_unused_columns=False,
)

reward_trainer = RewardTrainer(
    model=reward_model,
    args=reward_config,
    tokenizer=tokenizer,
    train_dataset=dataset,
    eval_dataset=eval_dataset,
)

reward_trainer.train()
reward_trainer.save_model("./reward_model_final")
print("Reward model training complete.")
    1. Run RLOO alignment on the policy
    from trl import RLOOTrainer, RLOOConfig
from transformers import pipeline

# Load the trained reward model as a pipeline for scoring
reward_pipeline = pipeline(
    "text-classification",
    model="./reward_model_final",
    tokenizer=tokenizer,
    device=0,
    torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16,
    truncation=True,
    max_length=512,
)

rloo_config = RLOOConfig(
    output_dir="./rloo_policy",
    num_train_epochs=1,
    per_device_train_batch_size=4,
    gradient_accumulation_steps=4,
    learning_rate=3e-6,
    kl_coef=0.05,           # beta: KL penalty coefficient
    rloo_k=4,               # number of responses sampled per prompt
    max_new_tokens=128,
    logging_steps=25,
    save_steps=200,
    bf16=True,
)

rloo_trainer = RLOOTrainer(
    config=rloo_config,
    tokenizer=tokenizer,
    policy=model,
    reward_model=reward_pipeline,
    train_dataset=dataset,
    eval_dataset=eval_dataset,
)

rloo_trainer.train()
rloo_trainer.save_model("./rloo_policy_final")
print("RLOO alignment complete.")
    1. Evaluate the aligned model
    from transformers import pipeline as hf_pipeline

aligned_pipeline = hf_pipeline(
    "text-generation",
    model="./rloo_policy_final",
    tokenizer=tokenizer,
    device=0,
    torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16,
)

test_prompts = [
    "Explain the difference between supervised and unsupervised learning.",
    "What is the capital of France?",
    "Write a short poem about the ocean.",
]

for prompt in test_prompts:
    output = aligned_pipeline(
        prompt,
        max_new_tokens=100,
        do_sample=True,
        temperature=0.7,
        pad_token_id=tokenizer.eos_token_id,
    )
    print(f"Prompt: {prompt}")
    print(f"Response: {output[0]['generated_text'][len(prompt):]}")
    print("---")
    1. Sanity-check: compare reward scores before and after alignment
    # Score a sample response with the reward model to verify improvement
test_response = "Supervised learning uses labeled data to train models, while unsupervised learning finds patterns in unlabeled data."

score = reward_pipeline(test_response)
print(f"Reward score for test response: {score[0]['score']:.4f}")
# A higher score after RLOO training vs. the base model indicates successful alignment

    Expected output¶

    After reward model training (~20 minutes), the reward model loss should decrease from approximately 0.69 (random) to below 0.55 on the eval set. After RLOO training (~40 minutes), the mean reward score on eval prompts should increase by 0.1–0.3 points relative to the SFT baseline, and the KL divergence from the reference model should stabilize between 2 and 8 nats. The aligned model's responses to the test prompts should be noticeably more structured and on-topic than the base Pythia-1B outputs.

    Common failure modes¶

    • CUDA out of memory during RLOO → Reduce rloo_k from 4 to 2, or reduce max_new_tokens from 128 to 64; RLOO's memory scales linearly with k.
    • Reward model outputs NaN scores → Ensure tokenizer.pad_token_id is set before loading the reward model; Pythia's tokenizer has no default pad token.
    • KL divergence explodes after 100 steps → Increase kl_coef from 0.05 to 0.1; the policy is drifting too far from the reference model.
    • Reward score does not improve → Check that the reward pipeline is receiving the full response (prompt + completion), not just the completion; the reward model was trained on full sequences.
    • RewardTrainer raises shape mismatch → Set remove_unused_columns=False in RewardConfig; the trainer needs the raw chosen and rejected columns.

    What comes next¶

    RLHF's KL-penalized objective has a closed-form solution that connects directly to the theory of optimal transport and energy-based models — understanding that connection makes the DPO reparameterization precise rather than surprising.

    • [[direct-preference-optimization]] — DPO reparameterizes the RLHF objective to eliminate the explicit RL loop, making the reward model implicit in the policy itself; the relationship is exact, not approximate.
    • [[proximal-policy-optimization]] — PPO is the RL algorithm that makes the RLHF training loop stable; understanding its clipping mechanism explains why RLOO can replace the critic but not the clipping.
    • [[reward-modeling]] — the reward model is the weakest link in the RLHF pipeline; this page covers Bradley-Terry training, reward model ensembles, and the scaling laws for reward model quality.

    Connected topics¶

    • [[transformer-architecture]] — RLHF post-training is applied almost exclusively to Transformer-based language models; the attention mechanism's capacity for in-context learning is what makes the SFT stage effective.
    • [[diffusion-models]] — RLHF-style preference optimization has been adapted for image generation, where reward models trained on human aesthetic judgments fine-tune diffusion policies using the same KL-penalized objective.

    Further reading¶

    • Ouyang et al. (2022) — "Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155 — the primary source for the three-stage RLHF pipeline; reading the appendix reveals the annotation guidelines and inter-annotator agreement statistics that are omitted from most summaries.
    • Ahmadian et al. (2024) — "Back to Basics: Revisiting REINFORCE-Style Optimization for Learning from Human Feedback" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740 — the RLOO paper; includes ablations comparing RLOO to PPO on reward score, KL divergence, and wall-clock time that are essential for practitioners choosing an RL algorithm.
    • Gao et al. (2023) — "Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760 — the first systematic empirical study of reward hacking as a function of KL divergence; establishes the quantitative relationship between policy-reference distance and proxy reward exploitation that every RLHF practitioner should internalize.
    • Rafailov et al. (2023) — "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290 — shows that the RLHF objective has a closed-form solution expressible entirely in terms of the policy, eliminating the need for explicit RL; the derivation is the clearest exposition of why the KL-penalized objective has the structure it does.
    • Lilian Weng — "Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback" (lil'log, 2023) — https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2023-01-27-the-transformer-family-v2/ — provides an intuitive walkthrough of the Bradley-Terry derivation and the reward hacking taxonomy that complements the mathematical treatment on this page.
    • Christiano et al. (2017) — "Deep Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences" — https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03741 — the conceptual origin of modern RLHF; demonstrates preference-based reward learning in Atari and MuJoCo environments, establishing the pairwise comparison framework that all subsequent work inherits.
