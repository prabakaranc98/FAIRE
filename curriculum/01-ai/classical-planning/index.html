    Classical Planning¶

    TL;DR: Classical planning is the formal problem of finding a sequence of actions that transforms a known initial state into a goal state — and in 2025, frontier LLMs have begun generating heuristics that outperform hand-crafted C++ solvers, making this the most contested boundary between symbolic AI and neural methods.

    What it is¶

    Here is the plot twist nobody in AI expected: in 2023, frontier language models scored absolute zero on basic planning benchmarks. Researchers at Apple published a paper titled "Can LLMs Really Reason and Plan?" and the answer, empirically, was no — GPT-4 failed tasks that a first-year AI student could solve by hand. The consensus hardened: planning requires symbolic rigor that neural networks fundamentally cannot provide. Then 2025 arrived. Gemini 2.5 Pro entered the International Planning Competition and, on several benchmark domains, outperformed state-of-the-art classical planners that had been tuned by domain experts over decades. The field has not recovered its consensus since.

    Classical planning, at its core, is the problem of finding a sequence of actions to get from where you are to where you want to be, given a complete and deterministic model of the world. The formalism that made this tractable — STRIPS, developed at SRI in 1971 — represents the world as a set of logical propositions, actions as precondition-effect pairs, and planning as a search through the space of reachable states. This is not a toy abstraction: the same formal structure underlies robot manipulation pipelines, logistics optimization at FedEx, and the action-selection layer in autonomous agents. What makes it hard is combinatorial explosion — a domain with 20 binary propositions has over a million possible states, and real logistics problems have billions.

    The reason classical planning survived the deep learning era is that it offers something neural networks historically could not: guarantees. A classical planner can certify that a plan is optimal (shortest possible), or that no plan exists, or that every action in the sequence is valid given the world model. These properties matter enormously in safety-critical settings. What is changing now is not the symbolic engine — the search algorithms and formal semantics are as rigorous as ever — but the heuristic guidance that tells the search where to look first. LLMs have turned out to be surprisingly good at generating that guidance, which is why the field is in the middle of a genuine paradigm shift.

    Why it matters at the frontier¶

    The frontier labs — Google DeepMind, Meta FAIR, and several academic groups — are investing heavily in classical planning not because they want to build better logistics schedulers, but because planning is the missing piece in autonomous agent architectures. A language model that can write code, browse the web, and call APIs is not an agent unless it can sequence those capabilities coherently toward a goal. The dominant approach in 2024 was to prompt LLMs to generate plans directly, but this produces plans that are syntactically plausible and semantically wrong — the model hallucinates preconditions, skips steps, and cannot detect when a plan has failed. Classical planning provides the verification layer: a symbolic executor that checks every action against the world model before committing to it.

    The deeper reason this matters is the optimality question. Satisficing planning — finding any valid plan — is tractable for modern systems, but optimal planning — finding the shortest valid plan — remains PSPACE-hard in general, and no LLM has demonstrated reliable optimality on non-trivial domains. This gap is not academic. In drug synthesis planning, the difference between a 12-step and an 8-step route is the difference between a viable and an unviable manufacturing process. In robotic manipulation, suboptimal plans accumulate error. The open question driving current research is whether the neuro-symbolic combination can close this gap, or whether optimality will always require pure symbolic search.

    Core concepts¶

    • State space — the set of all possible world configurations, represented as assignments of truth values to a finite set of propositions; planning is search over this space.
    • STRIPS representation — a formalism where each action has a precondition list (propositions that must be true), an add list (propositions made true), and a delete list (propositions made false); the foundation of nearly all classical planning languages.
    • PDDL (Planning Domain Definition Language) — the standard file format for specifying planning problems, separating the domain (action schemas) from the problem instance (initial state and goal); used in all International Planning Competitions.
    • Heuristic function — a function \(h(s)\) estimating the cost from state \(s\) to the goal; the quality of this estimate determines whether search is tractable or exponential.
    • Admissibility — a heuristic is admissible if it never overestimates the true cost to the goal; admissible heuristics guarantee that A* finds an optimal plan.
    • Relaxed planning graph — a polynomial-time-constructible approximation of the reachability structure of a planning problem, obtained by ignoring delete lists; the basis for the FF and h-add heuristics.
    • Satisficing vs. optimal planning — satisficing planning finds any valid plan (tractable in practice); optimal planning finds the shortest plan (PSPACE-hard in general, requiring admissible heuristics and exhaustive search).
    • Grounding — the process of instantiating lifted action schemas (with variables) into concrete ground actions (with specific objects); a preprocessing step that can itself be exponential in the number of objects.

    Mathematical foundations¶

    A classical planning problem is formally a tuple \(\Pi = \langle F, A, I, G \rangle\).

    \[ \Pi = \langle F,\; A,\; I,\; G \rangle \]

    where \(F\) is the finite set of propositional fluents (facts that can be true or false), \(A\) is the set of ground actions, \(I \subseteq F\) is the initial state (the set of fluents true at the start), and \(G \subseteq F\) is the goal condition (the set of fluents that must be true in the final state). This equation says that planning is entirely determined by these four components — no probabilities, no partial observability, no continuous dynamics.

    Each action \(a \in A\) is defined by three sets:

    \[ a = \langle \text{pre}(a),\; \text{add}(a),\; \text{del}(a) \rangle \]

    where \(\text{pre}(a) \subseteq F\) is the precondition (fluents that must hold for \(a\) to be applicable), \(\text{add}(a) \subseteq F\) is the add effect (fluents made true after applying \(a\)), and \(\text{del}(a) \subseteq F\) is the delete effect (fluents made false). The successor state after applying \(a\) to state \(s\) is \(\gamma(s, a) = (s \setminus \text{del}(a)) \cup \text{add}(a)\), defined only when \(\text{pre}(a) \subseteq s\).

    The h-max heuristic — one of the most theoretically important admissible heuristics — estimates goal distance by computing the maximum cost over all goal propositions:

    \[ h_{\max}(G, s) = \max_{p \in G}\; h_{\max}(\{p\}, s) \]

    where \(G\) is the set of goal propositions, \(s\) is the current state, and \(h_{\max}(\{p\}, s)\) is the estimated cost to achieve a single proposition \(p\). For a single proposition \(p \notin s\), this expands recursively as:

    \[ h_{\max}(\{p\}, s) = \min_{a \in A:\, p \in \text{add}(a)} \bigl\{ \text{cost}(a) + h_{\max}(\text{pre}(a), s) \bigr\} \]

    where \(\text{cost}(a)\) is the action cost (typically 1 for unit-cost domains), and the recursion bottoms out at \(h_{\max}(G, s) = 0\) whenever \(G \subseteq s\). This equation says that to achieve proposition \(p\), the planner finds the cheapest action that adds \(p\) and recursively estimates the cost of achieving that action's preconditions — taking the maximum over all goal propositions to get a single scalar estimate. The max aggregation makes h-max admissible (it never overestimates) but inadmissible in practice for guiding search efficiently, which is why h-add (summing instead of taking the max) is often preferred despite losing admissibility.

    Key algorithms / techniques¶

    • A* search — the canonical optimal planning algorithm; expands states in order of \(f(s) = g(s) + h(s)\) where \(g(s)\) is the cost from the initial state and \(h(s)\) is an admissible heuristic; guarantees optimality when \(h\) is admissible and consistent.
    • Greedy Best-First Search (GBFS) — expands states in order of \(h(s)\) alone, ignoring path cost; much faster than A* in practice but sacrifices optimality guarantees; the workhorse of satisficing planners like FF and LAMA.
    • FF (Fast Forward, Hoffmann & Nebel 2001) — the planner that made heuristic search dominant; uses a relaxed planning graph to compute the h-FF heuristic and combines GBFS with enforced hill-climbing; won the 2000 IPC and defined the next decade of research.
    • LAMA (Richter & Westphal 2010) — a multi-heuristic satisficing planner that iteratively improves plan quality using landmark-based heuristics; the most reliable satisficing planner on diverse benchmarks for over a decade.
    • Fast Downward (Helmert 2006) — the dominant open-source planning framework; implements causal graph heuristics, merge-and-shrink abstraction, and a modular architecture that most research planners build on; available at fastdownward.org.
    • SATPlan (Kautz & Selman 1992) — compiles a planning problem into a propositional SAT formula for a fixed horizon \(T\), then calls a SAT solver; if unsatisfiable, increments \(T\); optimal when the first satisfying assignment is found at the minimum \(T\).
    • LLM-generated heuristics (Anselm et al. 2025) — prompts a frontier LLM to write a Python function \(h(s) \to \mathbb{R}\) for a given domain, then plugs this function into a standard GBFS engine; demonstrated to outperform hand-crafted C++ heuristics on several IPC domains.
    • Landmarks — facts or actions that must appear in every plan from the initial state to the goal; computed by analyzing the causal structure of the planning graph; used as subgoals to decompose hard problems and as the basis for the LAMA heuristic.

    Current SotA¶

    On the IPC 2023 benchmark suite (the standard evaluation across 20+ domains), Fast Downward with the LAMA configuration remains the strongest classical planner for satisficing planning, solving over 85% of instances within a 30-minute timeout. For optimal planning, the Scorpion planner (using pattern database heuristics) leads on unit-cost domains. The dramatic 2025 development is that Gemini 2.5 Pro, when prompted with domain-specific context, matches or exceeds LAMA on several IPC domains in satisficing mode — the first time a neural system has reached this threshold on a controlled, adversarially-designed benchmark. Anselm et al. (2025) report that LLM-generated Python heuristics plugged into GBFS outperform the h-FF heuristic on 7 of 12 tested IPC domains, with the largest gains on domains requiring commonsense object relationships (Blocksworld variants, logistics) rather than geometric or numeric reasoning.

    What's happening now¶

    Research frontiers. The central research question has shifted from "can LLMs plan?" to "what is the right division of labor between neural and symbolic components?" Two competing architectures are emerging. In the heuristic generation paradigm (Anselm et al. 2025), the LLM writes a Python function that the symbolic search engine calls at every node expansion — the LLM contributes intuition, the engine contributes correctness guarantees. In the plan generation with verification paradigm, the LLM proposes a complete plan and a symbolic validator checks it, with the LLM iteratively repairing failures. The heuristic generation approach has shown stronger empirical results because it keeps the LLM in its strongest regime (generating short, structured code) rather than its weakest (maintaining coherent long-horizon state tracking).

    Engineering and systems. The practical bottleneck in deploying hybrid planners is latency: calling an LLM API at every node expansion in a search tree that may have millions of nodes is computationally infeasible. Current engineering solutions include caching heuristic evaluations for identical states, using the LLM to generate a batch of heuristic functions at problem-setup time and selecting among them, and distilling LLM-generated heuristics into lightweight neural networks that can be evaluated locally. The Fast Downward framework has become the standard integration target because its plugin architecture allows Python heuristics to be registered without modifying the C++ core. Several groups are working on PDDL-to-prompt compilers that automatically generate domain-specific prompts from PDDL files.

    Open problems. The most precisely stated open problem is: Can a neuro-symbolic planner guarantee \(\epsilon\)-optimal plans — plans within a factor \((1 + \epsilon)\) of optimal cost — on arbitrary PDDL domains, using an LLM heuristic that is not provably admissible, without falling back to exhaustive symbolic search? This is not a vague research direction — it is a formal question with a clear answer structure. A positive answer would require either a new theory of approximate admissibility for learned heuristics, or a mechanism for the LLM to certify bounds on its own estimates. A negative answer (showing that no such guarantee is possible without admissibility) would clarify the fundamental limits of the hybrid approach and redirect engineering effort toward satisficing-only architectures.

    In production¶

    • Amazon Robotics — uses classical planning (PDDL-based task planning) to coordinate pick-and-stow operations across fulfillment center fleets; the planning layer sequences robot arm actions with formal precondition checking to prevent collision states — aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning
    • Google DeepMind — AlphaCode 2 and subsequent systems use planning-style search over program synthesis spaces, with symbolic verification of intermediate states; the architecture is explicitly described as combining neural proposal with symbolic validation — deepmind.google/research
    • NASA JPL — the EUROPA planning framework, a descendant of classical planning research, has been used for Mars rover activity scheduling since the Spirit/Opportunity missions; the constraint-based temporal planner generates daily activity sequences with formal resource bounds — ai.jpl.nasa.gov
    • Siemens — uses PDDL-based planning for industrial process scheduling in manufacturing plants, with Fast Downward as the underlying solver; publicly documented in their AI research division's technical reports — siemens.com/global/en/company/innovation/research.html

    Minimum Valuable Build¶

    What you're building: A hybrid neuro-symbolic planner that uses Gemini Flash to generate a Python heuristic function, plugs it into a Greedy Best-First Search engine, and solves a Blocksworld task with plan validation.

    Why this build: This demonstrates the exact architecture that Anselm et al. (2025) showed outperforms classical C++ heuristics — you will see concretely how an LLM's commonsense knowledge translates into search guidance, and you will verify that the resulting plan is formally valid.

    Stack: Python 3.10+, google-generativeai 0.7+, no GPU required, runs on free Google Colab CPU.

    Estimated time: 45–60 minutes.

    The recipe¶

    1. Set up the environment

    Open a free Google Colab notebook. Install the Gemini SDK and set your API key (free tier is sufficient — Gemini Flash is the target model):

import google.generativeai as genai
import os

# Paste your Gemini API key here (get one free at aistudio.google.com)

    1. Define the Blocksworld planning problem

    Represent the world as a Python state (frozenset of true propositions) and define the action schemas manually. This is the STRIPS model in pure Python:

    # State: a frozenset of string propositions
# Goal: stack A on B on C (A on top, C on table)
# Initial: C on A, A on table, B on table — all clear except C

INITIAL_STATE = frozenset([
    "on(C,A)", "ontable(A)", "ontable(B)",
    "clear(C)", "clear(B)", "handempty"
])

GOAL = frozenset(["on(A,B)", "on(B,C)", "ontable(C)"])

BLOCKS = ["A", "B", "C"]

def get_actions(state):
    """Generate all applicable ground actions for the current state."""
    actions = []
    state_set = set(state)

    for x in BLOCKS:
        for y in BLOCKS:
            if x == y:
                continue
            # pickup x from y: x is clear, on(x,y), handempty
            if (f"on({x},{y})" in state_set and
                f"clear({x})" in state_set and
                "handempty" in state_set):
                pre = frozenset([f"on({x},{y})", f"clear({x})", "handempty"])
                add = frozenset([f"holding({x})", f"clear({y})"])
                delete = frozenset([f"on({x},{y})", f"clear({x})", "handempty"])
                actions.append((f"unstack({x},{y})", pre, add, delete))

            # stack x on y: holding(x), clear(y)
            if (f"holding({x})" in state_set and
                f"clear({y})" in state_set):
                pre = frozenset([f"holding({x})", f"clear({y})"])
                add = frozenset([f"on({x},{y})", f"clear({x})", "handempty"])
                delete = frozenset([f"holding({x})", f"clear({y})"])
                actions.append((f"stack({x},{y})", pre, add, delete))

        # pickup x from table
        if (f"ontable({x})" in state_set and
            f"clear({x})" in state_set and
            "handempty" in state_set):
            pre = frozenset([f"ontable({x})", f"clear({x})", "handempty"])
            add = frozenset([f"holding({x})"])
            delete = frozenset([f"ontable({x})", f"clear({x})", "handempty"])
            actions.append((f"pickup({x})", pre, add, delete))

        # putdown x on table
        if f"holding({x})" in state_set:
            pre = frozenset([f"holding({x})"])
            add = frozenset([f"ontable({x})", f"clear({x})", "handempty"])
            delete = frozenset([f"holding({x})"])
            actions.append((f"putdown({x})", pre, add, delete))

    return actions

def apply_action(state, action):
    name, pre, add, delete = action
    assert pre.issubset(state), f"Precondition violated for {name}"
    return (state - delete) | add

# Sanity check
actions = get_actions(INITIAL_STATE)
print(f"Applicable actions in initial state: {[a[0] for a in actions]}")
assert len(actions) > 0, "No actions available — check state definition"
    1. Prompt Gemini Flash to generate a Python heuristic

    Send the domain description and goal to the LLM and ask it to write a Python function. The key insight from Anselm et al. (2025) is that the prompt must include the state representation format so the LLM generates callable code:

    PROMPT = """
You are an AI planning expert. I have a Blocksworld planning problem.

The world state is a Python frozenset of string propositions like:
frozenset(["on(A,B)", "ontable(C)", "clear(A)", "handempty"])

The GOAL is: frozenset(["on(A,B)", "on(B,C)", "ontable(C)"])
Blocks are: A, B, C

Write a Python function with this exact signature:
    def heuristic(state: frozenset, goal: frozenset) -> float:

The function should return a non-negative float estimating how many
actions remain to reach the goal. Lower values mean closer to the goal.
Return 0.0 if and only if goal.issubset(state).

Use commonsense reasoning about block stacking. Count unsatisfied goal
conditions, penalize blocks that are in wrong positions, and reward
progress toward the target stack order.

Return ONLY the Python function code, no explanation, no markdown fences.
"""

response = model.generate_content(PROMPT)
heuristic_code = response.text.strip()

# Strip markdown fences if the model added them anyway
if heuristic_code.startswith("```"):
    lines = heuristic_code.split("\n")
    heuristic_code = "\n".join(
        line for line in lines
        if not line.startswith("```")
    )

print("=== LLM-Generated Heuristic ===")
print(heuristic_code)
    1. Execute the generated heuristic safely and validate it
    # Execute the generated code in a controlled namespace
exec_namespace = {}
exec(heuristic_code, exec_namespace)
heuristic = exec_namespace["heuristic"]

# Validate: heuristic should return 0 on the goal state
goal_state = frozenset(["on(A,B)", "on(B,C)", "ontable(C)",
                         "clear(A)", "handempty"])
assert heuristic(goal_state, GOAL) == 0.0, \
    "Heuristic must return 0.0 on goal state"

# Validate: heuristic should return > 0 on the initial state
h_init = heuristic(INITIAL_STATE, GOAL)
assert h_init > 0, "Heuristic must return > 0 on non-goal initial state"
print(f"Heuristic on initial state: {h_init:.2f} (expected > 0) ✓")
print(f"Heuristic on goal state: {heuristic(goal_state, GOAL):.2f} (expected 0.0) ✓")
    1. Run Greedy Best-First Search with the LLM heuristic
    import heapq

def greedy_best_first_search(initial, goal, get_actions_fn, apply_fn, h_fn):
    """
    Greedy Best-First Search.
    Returns the plan (list of action names) or None if no plan found.
    """
    # Priority queue: (heuristic_value, state, plan_so_far)
    frontier = [(h_fn(initial, goal), initial, [])]
    visited = set()
    nodes_expanded = 0

    while frontier:
        h_val, state, plan = heapq.heappop(frontier)

        if state in visited:
            continue
        visited.add(state)
        nodes_expanded += 1

        # Goal check
        if goal.issubset(state):
            print(f"Plan found! Length: {len(plan)}, Nodes expanded: {nodes_expanded}")
            return plan

        # Expand
        for action in get_actions_fn(state):
            name, pre, add, delete = action
            if state not in visited or True:  # always expand new states
                next_state = apply_fn(state, action)
                if next_state not in visited:
                    new_plan = plan + [name]
                    h_next = h_fn(next_state, goal)
                    heapq.heappush(frontier, (h_next, next_state, new_plan))

        if nodes_expanded > 10000:
            print("Search limit reached — heuristic may be uninformative")
            return None

    return None  # No plan found

plan = greedy_best_first_search(
    INITIAL_STATE, GOAL, get_actions, apply_action, heuristic
)

if plan:
    print("\n=== Plan Found ===")
    for i, action in enumerate(plan):
        print(f"  Step {i+1}: {action}")
else:
    print("No plan found.")
    1. Validate the plan by executing it symbolically
    def validate_plan(initial_state, goal, plan, get_actions_fn, apply_fn):
    """Execute the plan step by step and verify goal is reached."""
    state = initial_state
    print("\n=== Plan Validation ===")
    print(f"Initial state: {sorted(state)}")

    for i, action_name in enumerate(plan):
        # Find the matching action in the current state
        applicable = {a[0]: a for a in get_actions_fn(state)}
        if action_name not in applicable:
            print(f"INVALID: Action '{action_name}' not applicable at step {i+1}")
            print(f"  Current state: {sorted(state)}")
            return False
        state = apply_fn(state, applicable[action_name])
        print(f"  After {action_name}: {sorted(state)}")

    if goal.issubset(state):
        print(f"\n✓ VALID PLAN — Goal achieved: {sorted(goal)}")
        return True
    else:
        print(f"\n✗ INVALID PLAN — Goal not achieved.")
        print(f"  Missing: {sorted(goal - state)}")
        return False

is_valid = validate_plan(INITIAL_STATE, GOAL, plan, get_actions, apply_action)
assert is_valid, "Plan validation failed — check heuristic or search implementation"

    Stretch goals¶

    • Compare heuristics: Replace the LLM heuristic with a naive heuristic (count of unsatisfied goal propositions) and compare nodes expanded. The LLM heuristic should expand fewer nodes on this domain.
    • Harder problem: Extend to 5 blocks with a more complex goal stack. Observe how node count scales with and without the LLM heuristic.
    • Admissibility test: Add a parallel A* search and check whether the LLM heuristic is admissible by comparing plan lengths — if GBFS finds a longer plan than A*, the heuristic is inadmissible.

    Common failure modes¶

    • exec fails with SyntaxError → The LLM added markdown fences despite the prompt; the stripping code above handles this, but add print(heuristic_code) to inspect the raw output and strip manually if needed.
    • Heuristic returns 0.0 on non-goal states → The LLM wrote a trivial heuristic; re-run the prompt (Flash is stochastic) or add "do not return 0 unless goal.issubset(state)" to the prompt.
    • Search expands >10,000 nodes → The heuristic is uninformative; ask Gemini to revise it by including the current state and goal in the prompt and requesting a more discriminative function.
    • assert heuristic(goal_state, GOAL) == 0.0 fails → The LLM's goal check is wrong; add the exact goal frozenset to the prompt as a concrete example.
    • API quota exceeded → Switch to gemini-1.5-flash-8b (lower quota consumption) or cache the heuristic code string and skip the API call on re-runs.

    Code & implementations¶

    • Fast Downward — the standard open-source classical planning framework, implementing GBFS, A*, and dozens of heuristics; the integration target for most research planners: github.com/aibasel/downward
    • Unified Planning Framework (UPF) — a Python library from the AIPlan4EU project that provides a PDDL parser, planner interfaces, and plan validators; the cleanest Python API for classical planning: github.com/aiplan4eu/unified-planning
    • pyperplan — a lightweight educational Python planner implementing A* and GBFS with h-FF; useful for understanding the search loop without C++ complexity: github.com/aibasel/pyperplan
    • PDDL4J — a Java library for parsing and validating PDDL files; useful for building validators: github.com/pellierd/pddl4j
    • LLM-Heuristic Planning (Anselm et al. 2025) — the official repository for the paper demonstrating LLM-generated heuristics; includes the prompt templates and evaluation scripts: github.com/DillonZChen/goose

    What comes next¶

    Understanding classical planning's formal structure makes the heuristic design problem precise — the question is no longer "how do we guide search?" but "what makes a heuristic admissible, and when is admissibility worth the cost?"

    • Heuristic Search — the algorithmic foundation that classical planning sits on; A* and its variants are the engines that planning heuristics plug into.
    • Knowledge Representation — PDDL is one instantiation of a broader question about how to formally represent world states; description logics and ontologies extend this to richer domains.
    • Automated Reasoning — SATPlan connects planning directly to propositional satisfiability; understanding SAT solvers explains why compilation-based planners can be competitive with search-based ones.

    Connected topics¶

    • Transformer Architecture — Transformers are increasingly applied to solve or emulate symbolic classical planning tasks.
    • Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) — Both approaches aim to guide agent behavior toward desired goals using different paradigms.
    • Diffusion Models — Diffusion models can generate continuous trajectories, offering a modern alternative to classical planning.

    Further reading¶

    • Ghallab, Nau & Traverso — Automated Planning: Theory and Practice (2004), available at projects.laas.fr/planning; the definitive textbook covering STRIPS, PDDL, heuristic search, and temporal planning — more comprehensive than any survey paper.
    • Hoffmann & Nebel (2001) — "The FF Planning System: Fast Plan Generation Through Heuristic Search" — arxiv.org/abs/cs/0106025; the original FF paper with full derivation of the relaxed planning graph heuristic and empirical comparison across IPC domains.
    • Anselm et al. (2025) — "Classical Planning with LLM-Generated Heuristics: Challenging the State of the Art with Python Code" — arxiv.org/abs/2408.01744; the primary source for the LLM heuristic generation paradigm, with ablations showing which prompt components matter most.
    • Helmert (2006) — "The Fast Downward Planning System" — jair.org/index.php/jair/article/view/10457; the architecture paper for Fast Downward, explaining the SAS+ representation and causal graph heuristic that underlie most modern research planners.
    • Kautz & Selman (1992) — "Planning as Satisfiability" — proceedings available at cs.rochester.edu/~kautz; the original SATPlan paper demonstrating that planning problems can be compiled into SAT formulas, with the encoding that all subsequent SAT-based planners refine.
    • Lilian Weng's survey on model-based RL and planning (lil'log, 2019) — lilianweng.github.io/posts/2019-06-23-meta-rl; situates classical planning within the broader landscape of model-based sequential decision-making, useful for understanding where the boundaries between planning and RL are genuinely contested.
