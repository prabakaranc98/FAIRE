    Diffusion Models¶

    TL;DR: Generative models that learn to reverse a gradual noising process — currently the dominant paradigm for high-quality image, audio, video, and molecular structure generation, with a mathematical foundation that makes training stable and the framework applicable to almost any data modality.

    What it is¶

    Imagine dropping a single bead of blue ink into a glass of still water. Over minutes, the ink dissolves into the water until no trace of the original droplet remains — just a faint, uniform blue tint. Thermodynamics tells us this process is irreversible: you cannot un-mix the ink. Diffusion models turn this apparent impossibility into a tractable machine learning problem by breaking the reversal into hundreds of tiny, individually manageable steps. Instead of asking a neural network to conjure a photorealistic face from nothing, we ask it to answer a much simpler question at each step: given this slightly noisy image, what does a slightly less noisy version look like? Stack enough of those micro-decisions together, and you get generation.

    The mechanics work as follows. A forward process gradually injects Gaussian noise into a data sample over \(T\) timesteps until the original signal is completely destroyed and only noise remains. A neural network is then trained to reverse this process: given a noisy sample at timestep \(t\), predict either the clean signal or — more commonly in practice — the noise vector that was added. What makes this computationally tractable is a crucial mathematical shortcut: the forward process has a closed-form marginal distribution, meaning you can compute exactly what a sample looks like after any number of noising steps without simulating each step individually. Training therefore consists of sampling a random timestep, adding the appropriate noise in a single operation, asking the network to predict that noise, and computing a simple mean-squared error loss.

    The dominant instantiation of this framework — DDPM, introduced by Ho et al. in 2020 — established that predicting the added noise vector \(\epsilon\) rather than the clean image directly produces better training stability and sample quality. This noise-prediction objective connects diffusion models to score matching, the probabilistic framework that estimates the gradient of the log-density of the data distribution. That connection is not merely aesthetic: it explains why the iterative denoising procedure converges to samples from the true data distribution, and it opened the door to continuous-time generalizations that now underpin flow matching and stochastic differential equation (SDE)-based samplers.

    Why it matters at the frontier¶

    Diffusion models are the current answer to the question of how to generate high-quality samples from complex, high-dimensional distributions. Stable Diffusion, DALL-E 3, Sora, Stable Audio, and AlphaFold 3's structure diffusion module all rest on this framework. The reason diffusion displaced GANs as the dominant paradigm is training stability: there is no adversarial game, no discriminator to balance, no mode collapse. The reason flow matching is now challenging diffusion is inference speed — diffusion typically requires 50 to 1000 sequential denoising steps at inference time, while flow matching can achieve comparable quality in 8 to 20 steps by learning straighter probability paths. Understanding diffusion models is therefore not just understanding a technique; it is understanding the foundation from which the entire modern generative stack is either built or departing.

    The open problems in diffusion are not incremental. Inference acceleration remains unsolved at the theoretical level: current fast samplers (DDIM, DPM-Solver) are heuristic approximations, not principled derivations of the optimal trajectory for a given input. The relationship between the noise schedule and the geometry of the learned score function is poorly understood, which means practitioners tune schedules empirically rather than deriving them. And the question of whether pixel-space or latent-space diffusion is fundamentally superior — long assumed to favor latent models for efficiency — has been reopened by Hoogeboom et al. (2023), who showed that carefully optimized pixel-space models can match or exceed latent models on standard benchmarks. These are the questions that frontier labs are actively working on, and they are specific enough that a single paper could resolve any one of them.

    Core concepts¶

    • Forward process — a fixed Markov chain \(q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\) that adds a small amount of Gaussian noise at each of \(T\) steps, parameterized by a noise schedule \(\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_T\)
    • Closed-form marginal — \(q(x_t \mid x_0)\) can be computed in one step as a Gaussian with mean \(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\, x_0\) and variance \((1 - \bar{\alpha}_t)I\), where \(\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^t (1 - \beta_s)\); this is what makes training efficient
    • Reverse process — a learned Markov chain \(p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)\) that approximates the true posterior \(q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)\), parameterized by a neural network
    • Noise prediction (ε-prediction) — the dominant training objective, where the network predicts the noise vector \(\epsilon\) added to \(x_0\) to produce \(x_t\), rather than predicting \(x_0\) directly; empirically more stable
    • Score function — the gradient of the log-density \(\nabla_x \log p(x)\); the denoising network implicitly learns this at each noise level, connecting diffusion to score-based generative modeling
    • Noise schedule — the sequence \(\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_T\) controlling how fast signal is destroyed; linear schedules (DDPM) and cosine schedules (Improved DDPM) are standard; the choice dominates training dynamics more than architecture
    • Classifier-free guidance (CFG) — a technique for conditioning generation on a label or text prompt by jointly training a conditional and unconditional model, then interpolating their score estimates at inference; the standard method for text-to-image control
    • Latent diffusion — running the diffusion process in the compressed latent space of a pretrained VAE rather than pixel space, dramatically reducing compute while preserving perceptual quality; the basis of Stable Diffusion

    Mathematical foundations¶

    The forward process marginal. The key efficiency result is that you can compute \(x_t\) from \(x_0\) directly:

    \[ q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_t;\; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\, x_0,\; (1 - \bar{\alpha}_t) I\right) \]

    where \(x_0\) is the original clean data sample, \(x_t\) is the noisy version at timestep \(t\), \(\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t}(1 - \beta_s)\) is the cumulative product of signal-retention factors, and \(\beta_s \in (0, 1)\) is the noise variance added at step \(s\). This equation says that at any timestep, the noisy sample is simply a weighted mixture of the original data and pure Gaussian noise — the weights are determined entirely by the noise schedule.

    The reparameterization. From the marginal above, any noisy sample can be written as:

    \[ x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\, \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \]

    where \(\epsilon\) is the noise vector sampled independently at training time, and all other symbols are as above. This equation is the training workhorse: given \(x_0\) and a sampled \(\epsilon\), you can construct \(x_t\) for any \(t\) in a single matrix operation — no simulation of intermediate steps required.

    The DDPM training objective. Ho et al. (2020) showed that the variational lower bound on the log-likelihood simplifies to:

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{t,\, x_0,\, \epsilon}\!\left[\left\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\right\|^2\right] \]

    where \(t\) is sampled uniformly from \(\{1, \ldots, T\}\), \(x_0\) is a training sample, \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) is the noise added to produce \(x_t\), and \(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\) is the neural network's prediction of that noise given the noisy input and the timestep. This equation says that training is nothing more than supervised regression: the network learns to predict the noise it cannot see, given only the corrupted result. The simplicity of this objective — a plain MSE loss — is why diffusion training is so stable compared to adversarial methods.

    The reverse process posterior. The true reverse step has a tractable closed form when conditioned on \(x_0\):

    \[ q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_{t-1};\; \tilde{\mu}_t(x_t, x_0),\; \tilde{\beta}_t I\right) \]

    where \(\tilde{\mu}_t(x_t, x_0) = \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_t\) is the posterior mean, \(\tilde{\beta}_t = \frac{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\,\beta_t\) is the posterior variance, and \(\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t\). This equation says that the optimal denoising step is a specific linear combination of the current noisy sample and the predicted clean image — the network's job is to estimate \(x_0\) (or equivalently \(\epsilon\)) so this combination can be computed.

    The score function connection. The noise-prediction network is related to the score function by:

    \[ \nabla_{x_t} \log q(x_t \mid x_0) = -\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}} \]

    where \(\nabla_{x_t} \log q(x_t \mid x_0)\) is the score of the noisy distribution at \(x_t\), and \(\epsilon\) is the noise that was added. This equation says that predicting \(\epsilon\) is equivalent to estimating the score function scaled by the noise level — which is why diffusion models and score-based generative models are mathematically the same framework viewed from different angles.

    Key algorithms / techniques¶

    • DDPM (Ho et al., 2020) — the foundational pixel-space diffusion model; establishes ε-prediction, linear noise schedule, and the simplified MSE objective; 1000 inference steps
    • DDIM (Song et al., 2020) — deterministic sampler that reinterprets DDPM as a non-Markovian process, enabling 50-step inference with quality comparable to 1000-step DDPM; no retraining required
    • Improved DDPM (Nichol & Dhariwal, 2021) — introduces cosine noise schedule and learned variance, significantly improving log-likelihood and enabling fewer inference steps
    • Classifier-free guidance (CFG) (Ho & Salimans, 2022) — trains a single model to handle both conditional and unconditional generation; at inference, extrapolates beyond the conditional score for sharper, more prompt-faithful outputs; now standard in all text-to-image systems
    • Latent Diffusion Models (LDM) (Rombach et al., 2022) — moves the diffusion process into the latent space of a pretrained VAE, reducing image-space compute by ~16× while preserving perceptual quality; the architecture underlying Stable Diffusion
    • DPM-Solver (Lu et al., 2022) — a fast ODE solver for diffusion that achieves high-quality samples in 10–20 steps by exploiting the semi-linear structure of the diffusion ODE; widely used in production
    • EDM (Karras et al., 2022) — a principled framework that disentangles the network architecture, noise schedule, and sampler into independently tunable components; establishes best practices for training and sampling that most modern systems now follow
    • Simple Diffusion (Hoogeboom et al., 2023) — demonstrates that pixel-space diffusion with multiscale loss-weighting and simplified architecture matches or exceeds latent diffusion models in quality at high resolutions, reopening the pixel-vs-latent debate

    Current SotA¶

    On class-conditional ImageNet 256×256 generation, the EDM2 framework (Karras et al., 2023) achieves FID 1.91, the strongest result for diffusion-based models on this benchmark. For text-to-image generation, FLUX.1 (Black Forest Labs, 2024) — a rectified flow transformer trained at scale — achieves state-of-the-art human preference scores on GenEval and T2I-CompBench, outperforming DALL-E 3 and Stable Diffusion 3 on compositional prompts. In the pixel-space regime, Hoogeboom et al.'s Simple Diffusion (2023) demonstrates that a carefully optimized pixel-space model matches latent diffusion models on FID at high resolutions while requiring fewer architectural components, directly challenging the assumption that latent-space processing is necessary for efficiency.

    What's happening now¶

    Research frontiers. The most active theoretical question is whether the noise schedule and inference trajectory can be derived rather than chosen. Current schedules — linear, cosine, or EDM's log-normal — are validated empirically, but there is no principled theory for why one schedule produces better samples than another for a given data distribution. A related open question: can the optimal number of denoising steps be determined per-sample at runtime, rather than fixed globally? Consistency models (Song et al., 2023) and consistency distillation attempt to collapse the multi-step process into one or a few steps, but the quality-speed tradeoff remains worse than flow matching for most benchmarks. The Simple Diffusion result (Hoogeboom et al., 2023) has also reopened the pixel-vs-latent question at a theoretical level: if pixel-space models are competitive when loss-weighting is corrected, what does this imply about the role of the VAE latent space in prior work?

    Engineering and systems. Production diffusion inference has moved almost entirely to distilled, few-step models. Stable Diffusion 3.5 and FLUX.1 both ship with distilled variants that run in 4 steps using adversarial diffusion distillation or flow matching reflow. Quantization to INT8 and FP8 is now standard for serving, with NVIDIA's TensorRT and Hugging Face's diffusers library both supporting quantized inference pipelines. The architectural shift from UNet to DiT (Diffusion Transformer, Peebles & Xie, 2023) is nearly complete in frontier systems: FLUX.1, Stable Diffusion 3, and Sora all use transformer backbones, which scale more predictably with compute and integrate more naturally with text conditioning via cross-attention.

    Open problems. Three specific questions define the current research frontier. First: can we derive a method to dynamically adapt the inference trajectory and noise schedule for a specific input at runtime, rather than using a static, globally optimized schedule? This would require understanding the geometry of the learned score function per-sample, which is currently opaque. Second: what is the correct theoretical explanation for why ε-prediction outperforms x₀-prediction empirically, given that both are mathematically equivalent reparameterizations of the same objective? The empirical gap is well-documented but not theoretically explained. Third: can diffusion models be made to generalize compositionally — combining objects, attributes, and spatial relationships specified in text — without requiring explicit structured representations? Current text-to-image models fail systematically on compositional prompts (e.g., "a red cube to the left of a blue sphere"), and no diffusion-based approach has solved this without auxiliary supervision.

    In production¶

    • Google DeepMind — Imagen 3 powers text-to-image generation in Google's Gemini products; the system uses a cascaded pixel-space diffusion architecture with classifier-free guidance at multiple resolutions — research.google

    Minimum Valuable Build¶

    What you're building: A diffusion model trained from scratch on 2D Swiss Roll data that learns to reverse a Gaussian noising process and generate samples from a structured geometric manifold.

    Why this build: The Swiss Roll is the ideal first diffusion experiment — it is low-dimensional enough to visualize every step of the forward and reverse process, structured enough to verify that generation is working (the output should lie on a curved 2D manifold, not fill the plane), and fast enough to train in under three minutes on a free Colab T4. You will see, concretely, how a random Gaussian cloud morphs back into a structured shape through iterative denoising.

    Stack: Python 3.10+, PyTorch 2.1+, scikit-learn 1.3+ (for Swiss Roll data), matplotlib 3.8+

    Estimated time: 15–20 minutes including setup; training runs in under 3 minutes on a free Colab T4 or any modern CPU

    Compute: CPU is sufficient; free Colab T4 runs it in ~90 seconds

    The recipe¶

    1. Install dependencies and generate the Swiss Roll dataset
    # pip install torch scikit-learn matplotlib  (already available in Colab)
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.datasets import make_swiss_roll

device = torch.device("cuda" if torch.cuda.is_available() else "cpu")

# Generate 10,000 Swiss Roll points in 2D (drop the y-axis)
X_np, _ = make_swiss_roll(n_samples=10_000, noise=0.3)
X_np = X_np[:, [0, 2]]  # keep x and z axes
# Normalize to roughly [-1, 1]
X_np = (X_np - X_np.mean(axis=0)) / X_np.std(axis=0)
X = torch.tensor(X_np, dtype=torch.float32).to(device)

# Sanity check: should be (10000, 2)
assert X.shape == (10_000, 2), f"Unexpected shape: {X.shape}"
print(f"Data shape: {X.shape}, range: [{X.min():.2f}, {X.max():.2f}]")
    1. Define the noise schedule and forward process utilities
    T = 300  # number of diffusion timesteps
betas = torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T).to(device)          # linear schedule
alphas = 1.0 - betas
alpha_bars = torch.cumprod(alphas, dim=0)                   # ᾱ_t

def q_sample(x0, t, noise=None):
    """Add noise to x0 at timestep t using the closed-form marginal."""
    if noise is None:
        noise = torch.randn_like(x0)
    sqrt_alpha_bar = alpha_bars[t].sqrt().view(-1, 1)
    sqrt_one_minus = (1 - alpha_bars[t]).sqrt().view(-1, 1)
    return sqrt_alpha_bar * x0 + sqrt_one_minus * noise, noise

# Quick sanity check: at t=0, x_t should be close to x0
t_test = torch.zeros(5, dtype=torch.long).to(device)
x_noisy, _ = q_sample(X[:5], t_test)
assert x_noisy.shape == (5, 2)
print("Forward process check passed.")
    1. Define the denoising network (a simple MLP)
    class DenoiseMLP(nn.Module):
    """
    Predicts the noise ε given a noisy sample x_t and timestep t.
    Input: [x_t (2D), t_embedding (32D)] → hidden layers → ε (2D)
    """
    def __init__(self, hidden=256, t_dim=32):
        super().__init__()
        self.t_embed = nn.Embedding(T, t_dim)
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(2 + t_dim, hidden),
            nn.SiLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden, hidden),
            nn.SiLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden, hidden),
            nn.SiLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden, 2),
        )

    def forward(self, x, t):
        t_emb = self.t_embed(t)          # (B, t_dim)
        inp = torch.cat([x, t_emb], dim=-1)  # (B, 2 + t_dim)
        return self.net(inp)

model = DenoiseMLP().to(device)
print(f"Model parameters: {sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters()):,}")
# Expected: ~200,000 parameters
    1. Train with the DDPM simplified objective
    optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4)
batch_size = 512
n_epochs = 200

losses = []
for epoch in range(n_epochs):
    # Sample a random batch
    idx = torch.randint(0, len(X), (batch_size,))
    x0 = X[idx]

    # Sample random timesteps
    t = torch.randint(0, T, (batch_size,), device=device)

    # Forward process: add noise
    x_noisy, noise = q_sample(x0, t)

    # Predict noise
    noise_pred = model(x_noisy, t)

    # DDPM simplified loss: MSE between predicted and actual noise
    loss = ((noise_pred - noise) ** 2).mean()

    optimizer.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()

    losses.append(loss.item())
    if (epoch + 1) % 50 == 0:
        print(f"Epoch {epoch+1}/{n_epochs} | Loss: {loss.item():.4f}")

# Loss should decrease from ~1.0 to below 0.05
assert losses[-1] < 0.1, f"Training may not have converged: final loss = {losses[-1]:.4f}"
    1. Run the reverse process to generate new samples
    @torch.no_grad()
def p_sample_loop(model, n_samples=1000):
    """Generate samples by running the reverse diffusion process."""
    # Start from pure Gaussian noise
    x = torch.randn(n_samples, 2, device=device)

    for t_idx in reversed(range(T)):
        t_batch = torch.full((n_samples,), t_idx, dtype=torch.long, device=device)

        # Predict noise
        eps_pred = model(x, t_batch)

        # Compute the reverse step mean (DDPM formula)
        alpha_t = alphas[t_idx]
        alpha_bar_t = alpha_bars[t_idx]
        beta_t = betas[t_idx]

        coef = beta_t / (1 - alpha_bar_t).sqrt()
        mean = (1 / alpha_t.sqrt()) * (x - coef * eps_pred)

        # Add noise for all steps except the last
        if t_idx > 0:
            noise = torch.randn_like(x)
            x = mean + beta_t.sqrt() * noise
        else:
            x = mean

    return x.cpu().numpy()

samples = p_sample_loop(model, n_samples=2000)
print(f"Generated samples shape: {samples.shape}")  # (2000, 2)
    1. Visualize the result
    fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 5))

axes[0].scatter(X_np[:2000, 0], X_np[:2000, 1], s=5, alpha=0.5, c='steelblue')
axes[0].set_title("Real Swiss Roll data")
axes[0].set_aspect('equal')

axes[1].scatter(samples[:, 0], samples[:, 1], s=5, alpha=0.5, c='coral')
axes[1].set_title("Diffusion model samples")
axes[1].set_aspect('equal')

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig("swiss_roll_diffusion.png", dpi=150)
plt.show()
print("Saved: swiss_roll_diffusion.png")

    Expected output¶

    The generated scatter plot should show a curved, spiral-shaped manifold that visually matches the Swiss Roll structure of the training data. The samples should not fill the 2D plane uniformly — they should concentrate along the curved band. Training loss should decrease from approximately 1.0 at epoch 1 to below 0.05 by epoch 200. If you print samples.std(axis=0), it should be in the range [0.8, 1.5], similar to the training data's standard deviation.

    Common failure modes¶

    • Generated samples look like a Gaussian blob, not a Swiss Roll → Training has not converged; increase n_epochs to 400 or reduce batch_size to 256 to see more gradient updates per epoch
    • Loss stays above 0.3 after 200 epochs → Learning rate may be too high; try lr=1e-4; also verify that alpha_bars is monotonically decreasing (print alpha_bars[[0, 100, 200, 299]])
    • CUDA out of memory → Not expected for this model size, but if it occurs, move to CPU: device = torch.device("cpu")
    • Samples have the right shape but are scaled wrong → Check that the training data was normalized; X.mean() should be near 0 and X.std() near 1
    • assert losses[-1] < 0.1 fails → The MLP may need more capacity; increase hidden from 256 to 512

    • Hugging Face diffusers — github.com/huggingface/diffusers — the canonical production library; supports DDPM, DDIM, LDM, Stable Diffusion, FLUX, and 50+ schedulers; model IDs: stabilityai/stable-diffusion-2-1, black-forest-labs/FLUX.1-dev
    • EDM (Karras et al., 2022) — github.com/NVlabs/edm — NVIDIA's principled diffusion design space; reference implementation for EDM training and sampling
    • DiT (Peebles & Xie, 2023) — github.com/facebookresearch/DiT — Diffusion Transformer; the architecture underlying FLUX and Stable Diffusion 3
    • OpenAI improved-diffusion — github.com/openai/improved-diffusion — reference implementation for Improved DDPM (cosine schedule, learned variance)

    What comes next¶

    Score matching provides the probabilistic foundation that explains why the ε-prediction objective converges to samples from the true data distribution — understanding it makes the noise schedule in DDPM precise rather than heuristic.

    • [[score-matching]] — the probabilistic framework underlying ε-prediction; diffusion models are score matching applied at multiple noise levels simultaneously
    • [[flow-matching]] — generalizes the continuous-time perspective by learning straight probability paths rather than curved diffusion trajectories, achieving comparable quality in fewer inference steps
    • [[latent-diffusion-models]] — applies the diffusion process in the compressed latent space of a VAE, the architectural decision that made high-resolution generation computationally accessible

    Connected topics¶

    • [[score-matching]] — Diffusion models use score matching to estimate the gradient of the data distribution at each noise level.
    • [[flow-matching]] — Flow matching generalizes diffusion models by regressing vector fields directly, enabling faster generation with fewer steps.
    • [[transformer-architecture]] — Diffusion Transformers (DiT) replace traditional U-Net backbones with transformer architectures, now the dominant backbone in frontier text-to-image and video systems.

    Further reading¶

    • Ho et al. (2020) — "Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models" — arxiv.org/abs/2006.11239 — the primary source for the ε-prediction objective and the simplified training loss; read the appendix for the full ELBO derivation
    • Song et al. (2021) — "Score-Based Generative Modeling through Stochastic Differential Equations" — arxiv.org/abs/2011.13456 — unifies DDPM and score matching under a continuous-time SDE framework; essential for understanding the theoretical foundations
    • Karras et al. (2022) — "Elucidating the Design Space of Diffusion-Based Generative Models" — arxiv.org/abs/2206.00364 — the most rigorous treatment of noise schedules, network preconditioning, and sampler design; the reference for practitioners who want to understand why specific choices work
    • Lilian Weng — "What are Diffusion Models?" (lil'log, 2021) — lilianweng.github.io/posts/2021-07-11-diffusion-models — a thorough mathematical walkthrough of the DDPM derivation and its connections to score matching and energy-based models; useful as a companion to the original papers
    • Rombach et al. (2022) — "High-Resolution Image Synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models" — arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752 — the paper that introduced latent diffusion and Stable Diffusion; explains the VAE-diffusion interface and the cross-attention conditioning mechanism in detail
    • Hoogeboom et al. (2023) — "Simple Diffusion: End-to-End Diffusion for High Resolution Images" — arxiv.org/abs/2301.11093 — shows pixel-space diffusion is competitive with latent models at high resolution when multiscale loss-weighting is applied; directly relevant to the open question of whether latent space is necessary
