    Flow Matching¶

    TL;DR: A simulation-free training framework for continuous normalizing flows that learns straight-line vector fields from noise to data — achieving diffusion-quality generation in 8–20 inference steps instead of 50–1000, now powering FLUX, AlphaFold 3, and Voicebox.

    What it is¶

    Imagine two cities connected by a road network. A diffusion model is like navigating that network during rush hour — every particle takes a winding, congested route through high-dimensional space, doubling back, merging with other particles, and requiring hundreds of small steps to arrive at the destination. Flow matching is the highway bypass: it draws a straight line between where you start (a sample from Gaussian noise) and where you need to go (a data point), then trains a neural network to follow that line. The result is a generative model that can produce high-fidelity samples in a fraction of the steps.

    More precisely, flow matching trains a neural network \(v_\theta(x, t)\) to represent a vector field — a function that, at every point in space and every moment in time, says "move in this direction." Integrating this vector field as an ordinary differential equation (ODE) from \(t = 0\) to \(t = 1\) transports a noise sample into a data sample. The critical insight from Lipman et al. (2022) is that training this vector field does not require simulating the ODE at all. Instead, you construct a simple, analytically tractable conditional vector field between individual noise-data pairs — the straight line \(u_t(x \mid x_0, x_1) = x_1 - x_0\) — and regress against it directly. The marginal vector field that the model learns, averaged over all pairs, is provably consistent with the data distribution.

    This is what separates flow matching from earlier continuous normalizing flow (CNF) methods: those methods required integrating the ODE during training to compute the log-likelihood, which was computationally prohibitive. Flow matching bypasses this entirely. The training objective is a simple mean-squared error between the network's predicted vector field and the analytically computed conditional target. The consequence is that flow matching trains as cheaply as a standard diffusion model but produces straighter trajectories that require far fewer ODE steps to integrate accurately at inference time.

    Why it matters at the frontier¶

    The practical impact of flow matching became undeniable in 2024. Black Forest Labs released FLUX.1, an open-weight image generation model built on Rectified Flow (a flow matching variant) that outperformed DALL-E 3 and Stable Diffusion 3 on human preference benchmarks while running inference in 20–28 steps. AlphaFold 3 adopted a flow matching-like diffusion module as its structure prediction head, replacing the score-based diffusion used in earlier protein structure work. Meta's Voicebox and AudioBox use flow matching for zero-shot speech synthesis and audio generation. The convergence is not coincidental: flow matching's straighter paths translate directly into fewer neural function evaluations (NFEs) at inference, which is the dominant cost in production deployment.

    The theoretical significance runs deeper than engineering efficiency. Flow matching, score matching, and denoising diffusion probabilistic models are all instances of the same underlying framework — learning to transport one probability distribution to another. Flow matching makes this transport explicit and geometrically interpretable: the learned vector field is a velocity field on the space of probability measures, and the quality of generation is directly tied to the curvature of the learned paths. This geometric view has opened new research directions in optimal transport, Riemannian flow matching for non-Euclidean data, and discrete flow matching for language and protein sequences. Frontier labs are actively working on all three, and the theoretical tools are maturing fast enough that practical improvements are following within months of theoretical results.

    Core concepts¶

    • Continuous Normalizing Flow (CNF) — a generative model defined by an ODE \(\dot{x} = v_\theta(x, t)\) whose solution maps a simple source distribution to a complex target distribution; the flow is invertible by construction.
    • Vector field — a function \(v: \mathbb{R}^d \times [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^d\) that assigns a velocity to every point in space at every time; integrating it defines a trajectory (flow) through space.
    • Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) — the training objective that regresses the network against analytically computable per-sample conditional vector fields, avoiding ODE simulation during training.
    • Probability path — a time-indexed family of distributions \(p_t\) interpolating between the source \(p_0\) and target \(p_1\); the vector field generates this path by transporting mass continuously.
    • Optimal Transport (OT) coupling — a joint distribution over (noise, data) pairs that minimizes the expected squared distance \(\mathbb{E}[\|x_1 - x_0\|^2]\); using OT couplings instead of independent pairs straightens the learned trajectories.
    • Rectified Flow — a flow matching variant (Liu et al., 2022) that iteratively "reflows" trajectories by re-pairing noise and data using the model's own transport map, progressively straightening paths.
    • Neural Function Evaluations (NFEs) — the number of times the network \(v_\theta\) is called during ODE integration at inference; straighter paths require fewer NFEs for the same accuracy.
    • Interpolant — the time-indexed path \(x_t = \alpha_t x_1 + \sigma_t x_0\) connecting a noise sample \(x_0\) and a data sample \(x_1\); the choice of \(\alpha_t\) and \(\sigma_t\) determines path geometry and is a design choice.

    Mathematical foundations¶

    The flow matching training objective starts from the marginal flow matching loss, which is intractable directly but can be replaced by the conditional flow matching loss without changing the gradient:

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\, x_0 \sim p_0,\, x_1 \sim p_1} \left\| v_\theta(x_t, t) - u_t(x_t \mid x_0, x_1) \right\|^2 \]

    where \(v_\theta(x_t, t)\) is the neural network's predicted vector field evaluated at the interpolated point \(x_t\) and time \(t\), \(u_t(x_t \mid x_0, x_1)\) is the analytically known conditional target vector field for the specific pair \((x_0, x_1)\), \(x_0 \sim p_0\) is a noise sample drawn from the source distribution (typically \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\)), \(x_1 \sim p_1\) is a data sample drawn from the target distribution, and \(t \sim \mathcal{U}[0, 1]\) is a time uniformly sampled from the unit interval. This equation says that training is pure regression: at each step, sample a noise-data pair, interpolate to get \(x_t\), compute the analytic target velocity, and minimize the squared error.

    The linear interpolant (the simplest and most common choice) defines the interpolated point and its conditional target velocity as:

    \[ x_t = (1 - t)\, x_0 + t\, x_1, \qquad u_t(x_t \mid x_0, x_1) = x_1 - x_0 \]

    where \(x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is the point at time \(t \in [0,1]\) along the straight line from \(x_0\) to \(x_1\), \(x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is the noise sample, \(x_1 \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is the data sample, and \(u_t = x_1 - x_0\) is the constant displacement velocity along that line — independent of \(t\). This equation says that the conditional target is simply the displacement vector: constant along the path, the same at every timestep. The model learns to predict this displacement, averaged over all pairs in the dataset.

    The OT-CFM objective replaces independent sampling of \((x_0, x_1)\) with a minibatch OT coupling \(\pi^*(x_0, x_1)\) that minimizes expected squared displacement:

    \[ \pi^* = \arg\min_{\pi \in \Pi(p_0, p_1)} \mathbb{E}_{(x_0, x_1) \sim \pi} \left[ \|x_1 - x_0\|^2 \right] \]

    where \(\Pi(p_0, p_1)\) is the set of all joint distributions whose marginals are \(p_0\) and \(p_1\) respectively, \(\pi\) is a candidate coupling (joint distribution over noise-data pairs), and \(\pi^*\) is the optimal transport plan that pairs each noise sample with the data point it is geometrically closest to in expectation. This equation says that OT coupling minimizes the average path length, which in turn minimizes trajectory curvature and reduces the NFEs needed at inference.

    The continuity equation is the PDE that any valid probability path must satisfy:

    \[ \frac{\partial p_t}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (p_t\, v_t) = 0 \]

    where \(p_t : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\) is the probability density at time \(t\), \(v_t : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d\) is the vector field generating the flow at time \(t\), and \(\nabla \cdot\) denotes the divergence operator over the spatial coordinates. This equation says that probability mass is conserved: the vector field cannot create or destroy probability, only transport it. Flow matching implicitly satisfies this constraint by construction — the learned vector field generates a valid probability path by design.

    Key algorithms / techniques¶

    • Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) — Lipman et al. (2022); the foundational algorithm that replaces the intractable marginal flow matching objective with a tractable conditional regression loss, enabling simulation-free training of CNFs.
    • OT-CFM (Optimal Transport CFM) — Tong et al. (2023); augments CFM with minibatch OT couplings to straighten trajectories, reducing NFEs at inference without changing the training architecture.
    • Rectified Flow — Liu et al. (2022); independently derived flow matching with linear interpolants, plus the "reflow" procedure that iteratively re-pairs noise and data using the trained model to further straighten paths.
    • Multisample Flow Matching — Pooladian et al. (2023); uses minibatch OT couplings across multiple samples simultaneously to construct straighter flows, with theoretical guarantees on trajectory straightness.
    • Optimal Flow Matching — Kornilov et al. (2024); constructs displacement interpolants that provably minimize path curvature, achieving near-one-step generation on standard benchmarks.
    • Riemannian Flow Matching — Chen & Lipman (2023); extends CFM to Riemannian manifolds (spheres, tori, SO(3)) using geodesic interpolants, enabling flow matching on protein backbone angles and other non-Euclidean data.
    • Discrete Flow Matching — Campbell et al. (2024); adapts the CFM framework to discrete state spaces (tokens, graphs) by defining flows over categorical distributions, enabling flow-based language and protein sequence generation.
    • Carré du Champ Flow Matching — Bamberger et al. (2025); introduces geometry-aware anisotropic noise schedules derived from the carré du champ operator, resolving the quality-generalization tradeoff and preventing memorization on complex data manifolds.

    Current SotA¶

    FLUX.1-dev (Black Forest Labs, 2024), built on Rectified Flow, achieves a human preference win rate of 69% against DALL-E 3 on the GenAI-Bench benchmark and generates 1024×1024 images in 20–28 NFEs. On the ImageNet 256×256 class-conditional generation benchmark, flow matching models reach FID scores below 2.0 with 10–20 function evaluations, compared to 50–1000 for DDPM-class models at equivalent quality. For protein structure generation, AlphaFold 3's flow matching module achieves a median TM-score of 0.85 on CAMEO blind prediction targets (2024), surpassing all prior single-sequence methods.

    What's happening now¶

    Research frontiers. The most active theoretical direction is extending flow matching to non-Euclidean and discrete spaces. Riemannian Flow Matching (Chen & Lipman, 2023) handles smooth manifolds like protein backbone torsion angles, but the extension to combinatorial spaces — graphs, token sequences, molecular graphs with variable topology — requires fundamentally different interpolant constructions. Discrete Flow Matching (Campbell et al., 2024) and related masked diffusion approaches are converging on a shared framework, but the theoretical guarantees available for continuous flows (path straightness, OT optimality) do not yet transfer cleanly. Bamberger et al. (2025) introduced the carré du champ operator as a tool for constructing geometry-aware noise schedules, which opens the question of whether operator-theoretic tools from stochastic analysis can systematically resolve the quality-generalization tradeoff across data modalities.

    Engineering and systems. The dominant engineering challenge is reducing the cost of OT coupling computation at training time. Exact OT on a minibatch of size \(n\) costs \(O(n^3)\) with the Hungarian algorithm or \(O(n^2 / \epsilon)\) with Sinkhorn, which becomes a bottleneck at large batch sizes. Current practice uses approximate minibatch OT with Sinkhorn regularization (\(\epsilon \approx 0.05\)) and batch sizes of 256–1024, which is fast enough for image generation but may not scale to the trillion-token batches used in language model training. Several groups are exploring learned couplings — using a small auxiliary network to propose pairings — as a way to amortize the OT cost. On the inference side, consistency distillation applied to flow matching models (as in Stable Diffusion 3's distilled variants) can reduce NFEs to 4–8 with minimal quality loss, and this is now standard practice in production deployments.

    Open problems. The central unresolved question is: Can we mathematically guarantee and dynamically enforce that learned vector fields do not develop crossing paths or singularities when scaling to high-dimensional, hybrid discrete-continuous spaces, without incurring prohibitive OT matching costs at training time? Path crossings — where two trajectories from different data points pass through the same location at the same time — are provably impossible under exact OT couplings in continuous spaces, but the guarantees break down under minibatch approximations, in discrete spaces, and when the source and target distributions have overlapping support in complex ways. A related open question is whether there exists a principled way to choose the noise schedule (the interpolant \(\alpha_t, \sigma_t\)) for a given data modality without exhaustive empirical search — current choices (linear, cosine, learned) are largely heuristic and the sensitivity to this choice is poorly understood theoretically.

    Minimum Valuable Build¶

    What you're building: A from-scratch PyTorch implementation of Conditional Flow Matching with OT paths, trained on a 2D double-moon dataset, with real-time visualization of the learned vector field.

    Why this build: The double-moon dataset makes the vector field visually interpretable — you can literally watch the network learn to draw straight lines from noise to data, and see what happens when paths are not straight. This is the fastest way to develop intuition for why OT couplings matter.

    Stack: Python 3.10+, PyTorch 2.1+, matplotlib 3.7+, scipy 1.11+ (for OT coupling), numpy 1.24+. No GPU required — runs on free Colab CPU in under 2 minutes.

    Estimated time: 25–35 minutes end-to-end.

    The recipe¶

    1. Install dependencies and generate the dataset
    import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.spatial.distance import cdist
from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment

# Generate double-moon data (target distribution p_1)
def make_moons(n=2000, noise=0.05):
    n_half = n // 2
    theta0 = np.linspace(0, np.pi, n_half)
    theta1 = np.linspace(0, np.pi, n_half)
    x0 = np.stack([np.cos(theta0), np.sin(theta0)], axis=1)
    x1 = np.stack([1 - np.cos(theta1), 1 - np.sin(theta1) - 0.5], axis=1)
    X = np.concatenate([x0, x1], axis=0)
    X += np.random.randn(*X.shape) * noise
    return torch.tensor(X, dtype=torch.float32)

data = make_moons(n=4000)
print(f"Data shape: {data.shape}")  # (4000, 2)
assert data.shape == (4000, 2)
    1. Define the vector field network
    class VectorField(nn.Module):
    """MLP that maps (x, t) -> velocity in R^2."""
    def __init__(self, hidden=256):
        super().__init__()
        # Input: 2D position + 1D time = 3 dims
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(3, hidden),
            nn.SiLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden, hidden),
            nn.SiLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden, hidden),
            nn.SiLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden, 2),
        )

    def forward(self, x, t):
        # x: (B, 2), t: (B,) or scalar
        if t.dim() == 0:
            t = t.expand(x.shape[0])
        t_emb = t.unsqueeze(-1)  # (B, 1)
        xt = torch.cat([x, t_emb], dim=-1)  # (B, 3)
        return self.net(xt)

model = VectorField(hidden=256)
print(f"Parameters: {sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters()):,}")
    1. Implement minibatch OT coupling
    def ot_coupling(x0, x1):
    """
    Compute optimal transport coupling between minibatches x0 and x1.
    Returns reordered x0 such that (x0[i], x1[i]) are OT-paired.
    Cost matrix: squared Euclidean distance.
    """
    C = cdist(x0.numpy(), x1.numpy(), metric='sqeuclidean')
    row_ind, col_ind = linear_sum_assignment(C)
    return x0[row_ind], x1[col_ind]

# Sanity check: OT cost should be <= independent pairing cost
x0_test = torch.randn(64, 2)
x1_test = data[:64]
x0_ot, x1_ot = ot_coupling(x0_test, x1_test)
cost_ot = ((x1_ot - x0_ot) ** 2).sum(dim=-1).mean().item()
cost_ind = ((x1_test - x0_test) ** 2).sum(dim=-1).mean().item()
assert cost_ot <= cost_ind + 1e-4, "OT cost should not exceed independent cost"
print(f"OT cost: {cost_ot:.4f} vs independent: {cost_ind:.4f}")
    1. Write the CFM training loop
    optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4)
batch_size = 256
n_steps = 5000
losses = []

for step in range(n_steps):
    # Sample noise (source distribution p_0)
    x0 = torch.randn(batch_size, 2)

    # Sample data (target distribution p_1)
    idx = torch.randint(0, len(data), (batch_size,))
    x1 = data[idx]

    # OT coupling: pair x0 with nearest x1 in transport sense
    x0_paired, x1_paired = ot_coupling(x0, x1)

    # Sample time uniformly in [0, 1]
    t = torch.rand(batch_size)

    # Linear interpolant: x_t = (1-t)*x0 + t*x1
    t_col = t.unsqueeze(-1)  # (B, 1) for broadcasting
    xt = (1 - t_col) * x0_paired + t_col * x1_paired

    # Conditional target velocity: u_t = x1 - x0 (constant along path)
    ut = x1_paired - x0_paired

    # Predict velocity and compute MSE loss
    vt_pred = model(xt, t)
    loss = ((vt_pred - ut) ** 2).mean()

    optimizer.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()

    losses.append(loss.item())
    if step % 500 == 0:
        print(f"Step {step:5d} | Loss: {loss.item():.6f}")

print("Training complete.")
    1. Generate samples by integrating the learned ODE
    @torch.no_grad()
def generate(model, n_samples=1000, n_steps=20):
    """
    Euler integration of dx/dt = v_theta(x, t) from t=0 to t=1.
    n_steps=20 is sufficient for well-trained flow matching models.
    """
    x = torch.randn(n_samples, 2)
    dt = 1.0 / n_steps
    for i in range(n_steps):
        t = torch.full((n_samples,), i * dt)
        v = model(x, t)
        x = x + v * dt
    return x

samples = generate(model, n_samples=2000, n_steps=20)
print(f"Generated samples shape: {samples.shape}")  # (2000, 2)
    1. Visualize the vector field and generated samples
    fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(15, 4))

# Panel 1: Training data
axes[0].scatter(data[:, 0], data[:, 1], s=2, alpha=0.3, c='steelblue')
axes[0].set_title("Training data (double moon)")
axes[0].set_xlim(-2.5, 3.5); axes[0].set_ylim(-2, 2.5)

# Panel 2: Generated samples
axes[1].scatter(samples[:, 0], samples[:, 1], s=2, alpha=0.3, c='coral')
axes[1].set_title("Generated samples (20 Euler steps)")
axes[1].set_xlim(-2.5, 3.5); axes[1].set_ylim(-2, 2.5)

# Panel 3: Learned vector field at t=0.5
grid_x = np.linspace(-2.5, 3.5, 20)
grid_y = np.linspace(-2, 2.5, 20)
gx, gy = np.meshgrid(grid_x, grid_y)
grid_pts = torch.tensor(
    np.stack([gx.ravel(), gy.ravel()], axis=1), dtype=torch.float32
)
t_mid = torch.full((grid_pts.shape[0],), 0.5)
with torch.no_grad():
    vf = model(grid_pts, t_mid).numpy()
axes[2].quiver(
    grid_pts[:, 0], grid_pts[:, 1],
    vf[:, 0], vf[:, 1],
    alpha=0.7, scale=30, color='darkgreen'
)
axes[2].set_title("Learned vector field at t=0.5")
axes[2].set_xlim(-2.5, 3.5); axes[2].set_ylim(-2, 2.5)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig("flow_matching_result.png", dpi=150)
plt.show()
print("Saved: flow_matching_result.png")

    Expected output¶

    After 5000 training steps (roughly 90 seconds on CPU), the loss should drop from approximately 0.8–1.2 to below 0.05. The generated samples panel should show two clear crescent shapes matching the training data. The vector field panel at \(t = 0.5\) should show arrows pointing roughly from the center of the noise distribution toward the two moon clusters — visibly straighter than the curved fields you would see from a score-based model. If you reduce n_steps in generate() to 5, the samples should still be recognizable as double moons, demonstrating the efficiency of straight-path flow matching.

    Common failure modes¶

    • Loss plateaus above 0.2 after 2000 steps → The OT coupling on CPU with linear_sum_assignment is exact but slow for batch_size > 256; reduce to 128 or switch to Sinkhorn with scipy.sparse.csgraph.min_weight_full_bipartite_matching.
    • Generated samples form a single blob, not two moons → The network is underfitting; increase hidden dimension to 512 or add a fourth hidden layer.
    • AssertionError in OT sanity check → Floating point edge case; the tolerance 1e-4 handles this, but if it fires, check that x0_test and x1_test have the same batch size.
    • Quiver plot arrows all point the same direction → The model has not converged; run for more steps or check that t is being passed correctly (must be a 1D tensor of shape (B,), not a scalar).
    • Samples look good at 20 steps but noisy at 5 steps → OT coupling was not used or batch size was too small; verify the ot_coupling function is being called in the training loop.

    What comes next¶

    Score matching and flow matching are complementary views of the same transport problem — score matching estimates the gradient of the log-density, while flow matching estimates the velocity field directly; understanding both makes the connection to diffusion models precise.

    • [[score-matching]] — the probabilistic foundation that flow matching generalizes; the score function \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\) is recoverable from the flow matching vector field via the Fokker-Planck equation.
    • [[diffusion-models]] — the predecessor framework that flow matching largely supersedes for new architectures; the DDPM noise schedule is a special case of the flow matching interpolant with a specific \(\alpha_t, \sigma_t\) choice.
    • [[normalizing-flows]] — the model class that flow matching trains; understanding the change-of-variables formula and the continuous-time limit clarifies why simulation-free training is non-trivial.

    Further reading¶

    • Lipman et al. (2022) — "Flow Matching for Generative Modeling" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02747 — the original CFM paper; the appendix contains the proof that the conditional and marginal flow matching objectives have identical gradients, which is the theoretical core of the method.
    • Pooladian et al. (2023) — "Multisample Flow Matching: Straightening Flows with Minibatch Couplings" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14772 — derives the theoretical relationship between OT coupling quality and trajectory curvature, with quantitative bounds on NFE reduction.
    • Kornilov et al. (2024) — "Optimal Flow Matching: Learning Straight Trajectories in Just One Step" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.13117 — the clearest exposition of why displacement interpolants minimize curvature and how to construct them in practice.
    • Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden (2022) — "Building Normalizing Flows with Stochastic Interpolants" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15571 — establishes the stochastic interpolant framework that unifies flow matching, score matching, and diffusion under a single theoretical umbrella.
    • Lilian Weng's survey on flow-based deep generative models (lil'log, 2018, updated 2021) — https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2018-10-13-flow-based-deep-learning/ — provides the normalizing flow background that makes the CNF connection precise; read before the Lipman et al. paper if CNFs are unfamiliar.
    • Chen & Lipman (2023) — "Riemannian Flow Matching on General Geometries" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03660 — extends CFM to non-Euclidean manifolds; essential reading for anyone applying flow matching to molecular or structural biology data.
