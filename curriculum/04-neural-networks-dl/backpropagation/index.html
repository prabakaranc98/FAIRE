    Backpropagation¶

    TL;DR: Backpropagation is the dynamic programming algorithm that makes training deep networks computationally feasible — it computes exact gradients for every parameter in a single reverse pass by reusing cached forward activations, turning an otherwise intractable credit-assignment problem into an \(O(N)\) operation.

    What it is¶

    Imagine you need to update every weight in a 70-billion parameter language model after seeing a single training example. The naive approach — perturbing each weight by a tiny amount and measuring how the loss changes — would require running the entire forward pass 70 billion times, once per parameter. At even a millisecond per forward pass, that is roughly 2,200 years of compute per gradient step. This is the computational nightmare that backpropagation was designed to escape. Instead of measuring the effect of each weight independently, backpropagation asks a different question: given that the forward pass has already been run and every intermediate activation cached, can error signals be propagated backward through the network in a single reverse sweep? The answer is yes, and the key insight is the chain rule of calculus applied systematically in reverse topological order.

    Backpropagation works by decomposing the gradient of the loss with respect to every parameter into a product of local Jacobians. Each layer computes a simple local operation — a matrix multiply, a nonlinearity, a normalization — and during the forward pass, the inputs to each operation are cached in memory. During the backward pass, the algorithm walks the computation graph in reverse: it receives a gradient signal from the layer above, multiplies it by the local Jacobian of the current operation, and passes the result to the layer below. Because each local Jacobian is cheap to compute given the cached activations, the entire backward pass costs roughly the same as two forward passes. This is the dynamic programming insight: reuse what you already computed rather than recomputing it from scratch for each parameter.

    The algorithm was formalized by Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams in 1986, though precursors existed in control theory and automatic differentiation. What the 1986 paper demonstrated was that multi-layer networks trained with backpropagation could learn non-trivial internal representations — features that no human had hand-engineered — purely from data. That demonstration broke a decade-long stalemate in neural network research and established the template for every modern deep learning framework. Today, PyTorch and JAX implement backpropagation through automatic differentiation engines that build dynamic computation graphs, but the underlying algorithm is the same chain-rule recursion Rumelhart et al. described.

    Why it matters at the frontier¶

    Every gradient-based optimization method in deep learning — Adam, AdaFactor, Shampoo, Muon — depends on backpropagation to supply the gradient signal. This means that any limitation of backpropagation propagates directly into the training of frontier models. The most pressing current limitation is memory: storing all intermediate activations for a backward pass through a 70B-parameter transformer requires hundreds of gigabytes of GPU memory, which is why gradient checkpointing (selectively discarding and recomputing activations) has become a standard training technique. The tradeoff between memory and recomputation is an active engineering problem at every major lab, and it shapes decisions about model architecture, batch size, and hardware configuration.

    At the research frontier, backpropagation faces a deeper challenge: it is biologically implausible and architecturally rigid. The algorithm requires symmetric weight matrices between forward and backward passes (the "weight transport problem"), global synchronization across all layers before any update can occur (the "update locking problem"), and exact gradient information that is unavailable in physical neuromorphic hardware. These constraints have motivated a growing body of work on forward-only learning rules — perturbation-based methods, forward-forward algorithms, and local learning rules — that attempt to match backpropagation's sample efficiency without its structural requirements. Whether any of these alternatives can scale to the parameter counts where backpropagation currently operates remains an open question that several labs are actively investigating.

    Core concepts¶

    • Credit assignment — the problem of determining how much each weight in a network contributed to the final loss, which backpropagation solves by propagating error signals backward through the computation graph.
    • Computation graph — a directed acyclic graph where nodes are operations and edges carry tensors; backpropagation traverses this graph in reverse topological order.
    • Local Jacobian — the matrix of partial derivatives of a layer's outputs with respect to its inputs, computed cheaply from cached forward activations and used to route gradient signals.
    • Chain rule — the calculus identity \(\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{dx} = \frac{d\mathcal{L}}{dy} \cdot \frac{dy}{dx}\) that allows gradients to be composed across layers without recomputing the full derivative from scratch.
    • Gradient accumulation — the practice of summing gradients over multiple forward passes before applying a weight update, used to simulate large batch sizes on memory-constrained hardware.
    • Gradient checkpointing — a memory-reduction technique that discards intermediate activations during the forward pass and recomputes them on demand during the backward pass, trading compute for memory.
    • Vanishing gradient — the phenomenon where gradient magnitudes shrink exponentially as they propagate through many layers, making early-layer weights learn extremely slowly; addressed by careful initialization, normalization, and activation function choice.
    • Jacobian-vector product (JVP) — the efficient computation of the product of a Jacobian matrix with a vector without materializing the full Jacobian, which is how modern autograd engines implement backward passes in \(O(N)\) time.

    Mathematical foundations¶

    The forward pass of a two-layer network with parameters \(\mathbf{W}^{(1)}, \mathbf{W}^{(2)}\) computes:

    \[ \mathbf{z}^{(1)} = \mathbf{W}^{(1)} \mathbf{x}, \quad \mathbf{a}^{(1)} = \sigma(\mathbf{z}^{(1)}), \quad \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{W}^{(2)} \mathbf{a}^{(1)} \]

    where \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) is the input vector, \(\mathbf{W}^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times d}\) and \(\mathbf{W}^{(2)} \in \mathbb{R}^{c \times h}\) are weight matrices, \(\sigma\) is an elementwise nonlinearity (e.g., ReLU), \(\mathbf{a}^{(1)}\) is the hidden activation, and \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) is the network output. This equation says that the network is a composition of affine maps and pointwise nonlinearities, and every intermediate value must be cached for the backward pass.

    The scalar loss \(\mathcal{L}\) measures the discrepancy between \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) and the true label \(\mathbf{y}\). The gradient with respect to the output-layer weights follows directly:

    \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(2)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)} \left(\mathbf{a}^{(1)}\right)^\top \]

    where \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{y}}}\) is the upstream gradient arriving from the loss function (a column vector in \(\mathbb{R}^{c}\)), and \(\mathbf{a}^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{h}\) is the cached hidden activation from the forward pass. This equation says that the gradient of the loss with respect to a weight matrix is an outer product of the upstream error signal and the layer's input — a rank-one update that can be computed in \(O(ch)\) time.

    The backward pass through the hidden layer applies the chain rule through the nonlinearity:

    \[ \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)} = \left(\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\right)^\top \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)} \odot \sigma'(\mathbf{z}^{(1)}) \]

    where \(\left(\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\right)^\top \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)}\) routes the upstream gradient back through the weight matrix, \(\sigma'(\mathbf{z}^{(1)})\) is the elementwise derivative of the activation function evaluated at the cached pre-activation \(\mathbf{z}^{(1)}\), and \(\odot\) denotes elementwise multiplication. This equation says that the gradient signal is first linearly transformed by the transposed weight matrix, then gated by how sensitive the nonlinearity was at each unit — units that were saturated (e.g., in the flat region of a sigmoid) pass almost no gradient backward.

    The gradient with respect to the first-layer weights then follows the same outer-product pattern:

    \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(1)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)} \mathbf{x}^\top \]

    where \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{h}\) is the error signal computed above and \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) is the original input cached during the forward pass. This equation says that the gradient of the loss with respect to the first layer's weights depends on the input to that layer and the error signal that arrived from all layers above it — the entire credit-assignment chain collapses into a single outer product.

    Key algorithms / techniques¶

    • Vanilla backpropagation (Rumelhart et al., 1986) — the original formulation: run a forward pass, cache all activations, walk the computation graph in reverse applying the chain rule, accumulate parameter gradients, and apply a gradient descent update.
    • Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with momentum — pairs backpropagation with a momentum buffer that accumulates a running average of past gradients, smoothing the update direction and accelerating convergence in ravines.
    • Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015) — adapts the learning rate per parameter using first and second moment estimates of the gradient, making it robust to sparse gradients and ill-conditioned loss surfaces; the default optimizer for most transformer training runs.
    • Gradient checkpointing (Chen et al., 2016) — divides the computation graph into segments, discards activations between segment boundaries during the forward pass, and recomputes them on demand during the backward pass; reduces activation memory from \(O(N)\) to \(O(\sqrt{N})\) at the cost of one additional forward pass.
    • Mixed-precision training (Micikevicius et al., 2018) — stores weights in FP32 but computes forward and backward passes in FP16 or BF16, halving memory bandwidth requirements while maintaining numerical stability through a loss-scaling trick that prevents gradient underflow.
    • Gradient clipping — caps the global norm of the gradient vector before applying the update, preventing the exploding gradient problem that destabilizes training in deep recurrent networks and transformers.
    • Reverse-mode automatic differentiation — the general framework that implements backpropagation for arbitrary computation graphs by recording operations during the forward pass and replaying them in reverse; the foundation of PyTorch's autograd and JAX's grad transform.

    Current SotA¶

    Backpropagation itself is not benchmarked directly — it is the substrate on which all benchmarked systems run. The relevant frontier is memory-efficient gradient computation. FlashAttention-3 (Shah et al., 2024) achieves 1.5–2× speedup over FlashAttention-2 on H100 GPUs by fusing the forward and backward attention kernels to minimize HBM reads, enabling training of 70B-parameter models with sequence lengths exceeding 128K tokens. Gradient checkpointing combined with activation offloading to CPU RAM, as implemented in DeepSpeed ZeRO-3, allows training of models exceeding 100B parameters on clusters of A100 80GB GPUs without model parallelism. The practical limit of backpropagation at scale is now memory bandwidth and inter-device communication, not the algorithm itself.

    What's happening now¶

    Research frontiers. The most active theoretical challenge to backpropagation is the forward-forward algorithm (Hinton, 2022), which proposes replacing the backward pass entirely with two forward passes — one on real data and one on synthetically corrupted data — using a local contrastive objective at each layer. Early results show competitive performance on MNIST and CIFAR-10, but the method has not yet matched backpropagation on large-scale benchmarks. Separately, perturbation-based methods like PEPITA and direct feedback alignment attempt to sidestep the weight transport problem by using random fixed feedback matrices instead of transposed forward weights, accepting some gradient approximation error in exchange for biological plausibility. Neither approach has demonstrated the sample efficiency of exact backpropagation at scale.

    Engineering and systems. The engineering frontier is dominated by making backpropagation faster and cheaper on modern accelerators. Compiler-level fusion — implemented in torch.compile (PyTorch 2.0+) and XLA (JAX) — eliminates kernel launch overhead by fusing sequences of elementwise operations in the backward pass into single GPU kernels. Quantized training (e.g., FP8 gradients as in Transformer Engine) pushes memory bandwidth further, though numerical stability of FP8 backward passes remains an active engineering problem. Distributed training frameworks like Megatron-LM and DeepSpeed implement pipeline parallelism that overlaps forward passes of one micro-batch with backward passes of another, hiding the sequential dependency that would otherwise leave GPUs idle.

    Open problems. The weight transport problem remains unsolved at scale: how can a physical system — whether a neuromorphic chip or a biological neural circuit — implement backpropagation-level credit assignment without requiring that the feedback pathway use exactly the transposed weights of the forward pathway? Formally, the question is whether there exists a local learning rule \(f(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{e}_i)\) — depending only on a neuron's local activation \(\mathbf{x}_i\) and some local error signal \(\mathbf{e}_i\) — that converges to the same fixed point as gradient descent on the global loss, without any global synchronization step. Current approximations (feedback alignment, target propagation) converge to different fixed points and lose accuracy on deep networks. A proof of impossibility or a constructive algorithm would be a significant theoretical result.

    In production¶

    • Meta AI — trains Llama 3 (8B–405B parameters) using backpropagation with FSDP (Fully Sharded Data Parallelism) across thousands of H100 GPUs; gradient sharding reduces per-device memory by a factor equal to the number of data-parallel workers — ai.meta.com/research/publications/the-llama-3-herd-of-models/
    • Google DeepMind — trains Gemini Ultra using a combination of tensor parallelism and pipeline parallelism with gradient checkpointing; the backward pass is distributed across TPU pods with custom XLA kernels that fuse attention backward operations — research.google/blog/gemini/
    • OpenAI — GPT-4 training used mixed-precision backpropagation (BF16 forward/backward, FP32 master weights) with gradient clipping at global norm 1.0; the training infrastructure is described in the GPT-4 technical report — openai.com/research/gpt-4
    • NVIDIA — Megatron-LM implements interleaved pipeline schedules that overlap backward passes of one pipeline stage with forward passes of the next, achieving up to 50% reduction in pipeline bubble overhead for 530B-parameter models — developer.nvidia.com/blog/scaling-language-model-training-to-a-trillion-parameters-using-megatron/

    Minimum Valuable Build¶

    What you're building: A two-layer MLP trained on MNIST using hand-written backpropagation in NumPy — no PyTorch autograd, no Keras, no automatic differentiation of any kind.

    Why this build: Writing the backward pass by hand forces you to confront exactly what loss.backward() is doing: caching activations, computing local Jacobians, and accumulating outer-product gradient updates. After this build, reading PyTorch's autograd source code or a paper's gradient derivation will feel concrete rather than abstract.

    Stack: Python 3.10+, NumPy 1.26+, scikit-learn 1.4+ (for MNIST loading only)

    Estimated time: 60–90 minutes

    The recipe¶

    1. Install dependencies and load MNIST
    pip install numpy scikit-learn

    import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import fetch_openml
from sklearn.preprocessing import OneHotEncoder

# Load MNIST (70,000 samples, 784 features)
mnist = fetch_openml("mnist_784", version=1, as_frame=False)
X, y = mnist.data / 255.0, mnist.target.astype(int)

# Train/test split
X_train, X_test = X[:60000], X[60000:]
y_train, y_test = y[:60000], y[60000:]

# One-hot encode labels
enc = OneHotEncoder(sparse_output=False)
Y_train = enc.fit_transform(y_train.reshape(-1, 1))  # (60000, 10)
Y_test  = enc.transform(y_test.reshape(-1, 1))

print(f"X_train: {X_train.shape}, Y_train: {Y_train.shape}")
# Expected: X_train: (60000, 784), Y_train: (60000, 10)
    1. Initialize weights with Xavier scaling
    np.random.seed(42)

d_in, d_hidden, d_out = 784, 256, 10

# Xavier initialization: scale by sqrt(2 / fan_in + fan_out)
W1 = np.random.randn(d_in, d_hidden) * np.sqrt(2.0 / (d_in + d_hidden))
b1 = np.zeros((1, d_hidden))

W2 = np.random.randn(d_hidden, d_out) * np.sqrt(2.0 / (d_hidden + d_out))
b2 = np.zeros((1, d_out))

# Sanity check shapes
assert W1.shape == (784, 256)
assert W2.shape == (256, 10)
print("Weights initialized.")
    1. Define the forward pass — cache everything
    def relu(z):
    return np.maximum(0, z)

def relu_grad(z):
    return (z > 0).astype(float)

def softmax(z):
    # Numerically stable: subtract row max before exp
    z_shifted = z - z.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    exp_z = np.exp(z_shifted)
    return exp_z / exp_z.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

def cross_entropy_loss(probs, Y):
    # Clip to avoid log(0)
    eps = 1e-9
    return -np.mean(np.sum(Y * np.log(probs + eps), axis=1))

def forward(X, W1, b1, W2, b2):
    """Returns predictions and a cache of intermediate values."""
    Z1 = X @ W1 + b1          # (N, 256)
    A1 = relu(Z1)              # (N, 256)
    Z2 = A1 @ W2 + b2          # (N, 10)
    probs = softmax(Z2)        # (N, 10)
    cache = {"X": X, "Z1": Z1, "A1": A1, "Z2": Z2}
    return probs, cache
    1. Write the backward pass by hand
    def backward(probs, Y, cache, W1, W2):
    """
    Computes gradients for W1, b1, W2, b2 using the chain rule.

    Backward through softmax + cross-entropy:
      dL/dZ2 = (probs - Y) / N
    Backward through W2:
      dL/dW2 = A1.T @ dZ2
    Backward through ReLU:
      dZ1 = (dZ2 @ W2.T) * relu_grad(Z1)
    Backward through W1:
      dL/dW1 = X.T @ dZ1
    """
    N = Y.shape[0]
    X, Z1, A1 = cache["X"], cache["Z1"], cache["A1"]

    # Gradient of loss w.r.t. softmax input (combined softmax + CE gradient)
    dZ2 = (probs - Y) / N                   # (N, 10)

    # Gradients for output layer
    dW2 = A1.T @ dZ2                         # (256, 10)
    db2 = dZ2.sum(axis=0, keepdims=True)     # (1, 10)

    # Backprop through ReLU hidden layer
    dA1 = dZ2 @ W2.T                         # (N, 256)
    dZ1 = dA1 * relu_grad(Z1)               # (N, 256) — gate by ReLU derivative

    # Gradients for input layer
    dW1 = X.T @ dZ1                          # (784, 256)
    db1 = dZ1.sum(axis=0, keepdims=True)     # (1, 256)

    return dW1, db1, dW2, db2
    1. Train with mini-batch SGD
    lr = 0.05
batch_size = 256
epochs = 20
n_train = X_train.shape[0]

for epoch in range(epochs):
    # Shuffle training data
    idx = np.random.permutation(n_train)
    X_shuf, Y_shuf = X_train[idx], Y_train[idx]

    epoch_loss = 0.0
    n_batches = 0

    for start in range(0, n_train, batch_size):
        Xb = X_shuf[start:start + batch_size]
        Yb = Y_shuf[start:start + batch_size]

        # Forward
        probs, cache = forward(Xb, W1, b1, W2, b2)
        loss = cross_entropy_loss(probs, Yb)
        epoch_loss += loss
        n_batches += 1

        # Backward
        dW1, db1_grad, dW2, db2_grad = backward(probs, Yb, cache, W1, W2)

        # SGD update (in-place)
        W1 -= lr * dW1
        b1 -= lr * db1_grad
        W2 -= lr * dW2
        b2 -= lr * db2_grad

    # Evaluate on test set
    probs_test, _ = forward(X_test, W1, b1, W2, b2)
    preds = probs_test.argmax(axis=1)
    acc = (preds == y_test).mean()
    print(f"Epoch {epoch+1:02d} | loss: {epoch_loss/n_batches:.4f} | test acc: {acc:.4f}")
    1. Verify gradients numerically (optional but instructive)
    def numerical_gradient(X, Y, W1, b1, W2, b2, eps=1e-5):
    """Finite-difference check on a single weight in W1."""
    probs, _ = forward(X, W1, b1, W2, b2)
    loss_base = cross_entropy_loss(probs, Y)

    # Perturb W1[0, 0]
    W1_plus = W1.copy(); W1_plus[0, 0] += eps
    probs_plus, _ = forward(X, W1_plus, b1, W2, b2)
    loss_plus = cross_entropy_loss(probs_plus, Y)

    numerical_grad = (loss_plus - loss_base) / eps

    # Analytic gradient
    probs_base, cache = forward(X, W1, b1, W2, b2)
    dW1, *_ = backward(probs_base, Y, cache, W1, W2)
    analytic_grad = dW1[0, 0]

    print(f"Numerical:  {numerical_grad:.8f}")
    print(f"Analytic:   {analytic_grad:.8f}")
    print(f"Relative error: {abs(numerical_grad - analytic_grad) / (abs(numerical_grad) + abs(analytic_grad)):.2e}")

# Run on a small batch
numerical_gradient(X_train[:32], Y_train[:32], W1, b1, W2, b2)

    Common failure modes¶

    • Test accuracy stuck at ~10% (random chance) → The softmax-CE combined gradient (probs - Y) / N is wrong; check that Y is one-hot encoded and that the division by N is present.
    • Loss is NaN after epoch 1 → Learning rate is too high; reduce from 0.05 to 0.01. Also check that the softmax implementation subtracts the row maximum before exp.
    • Gradient check relative error > 1e-3 → The ReLU derivative is using >= instead of >; change relu_grad to (z > 0).astype(float).
    • Memory error on large batches → Reduce batch_size from 256 to 64; the outer-product gradient matrices (N, 784) and (N, 256) are large.
    • Weights not updating → Verify that W1 -= lr * dW1 is modifying the global W1, not a local copy; avoid W1 = W1 - lr * dW1 inside a function scope without returning the updated weights.

    Code & implementations¶

    • PyTorch autograd internals — github.com/pytorch/pytorch/tree/main/torch/autograd — the production implementation of reverse-mode AD that powers loss.backward(); reading engine.py alongside this page makes the caching and graph traversal concrete.
    • JAX grad transform — github.com/google/jax — implements backpropagation through function composition rather than a mutable graph; the jax.grad and jax.vjp (vector-Jacobian product) APIs expose the underlying JVP/VJP machinery directly.
    • micrograd (Karpathy) — github.com/karpathy/micrograd — a 100-line scalar-valued autograd engine that builds a dynamic computation graph and runs backpropagation; the cleanest reference implementation for understanding the graph traversal.
    • tinygrad — github.com/tinygrad/tinygrad — a minimal tensor autograd library that extends micrograd to tensor operations; useful for understanding how the scalar chain rule generalizes to batched Jacobian-vector products.

    What comes next¶

    Understanding backpropagation makes the behavior of optimizers precise rather than heuristic — the gradient that Adam or SGD consumes is exactly what the backward pass produces, and knowing its structure explains why certain architectures train faster than others.

    • Optimizers and gradient descent — the algorithms that consume backpropagation's gradient signal; understanding the gradient's structure (sparsity, curvature, noise) explains why Adam outperforms SGD on transformers.
    • Automatic differentiation — the general framework that implements backpropagation for arbitrary computation graphs; forward-mode AD (JVPs) and reverse-mode AD (VJPs) are the two dual algorithms, and backpropagation is the special case of reverse-mode applied to scalar losses.
    • Vanishing and exploding gradients — the failure modes that emerge when backpropagation propagates through many layers; understanding the chain-rule product structure explains why residual connections and normalization layers are necessary.

    Connected topics¶

    • Data Parallelism — Data parallelism distributes the computation of gradients during backpropagation across multiple devices.
    • Transformer Architecture — Transformer architectures are deep neural networks trained using backpropagation to learn attention weights.
    • Diffusion Models — Diffusion models use backpropagation to train neural networks that reverse the diffusion process.
    • Flow Matching — Flow matching optimizes vector fields using backpropagation to train continuous-time generative models.
    • Score Matching — Score matching provides objectives optimized via backpropagation to train energy-based and score models.
    • Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) — RLHF uses backpropagation to optimize policy networks based on learned reward models.

    Further reading¶

    • Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams (1986) — "Learning representations by back-propagating errors" — https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986Natur.323..533R/abstract — the original paper; reading the four-page Nature letter alongside the MVB above makes the 1986 formulation and the NumPy implementation directly comparable.
    • Baydin et al. (2018) — "Automatic differentiation in machine learning: a survey" — https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05767 — situates backpropagation within the broader landscape of forward-mode and reverse-mode AD, clarifying why reverse-mode is preferred for scalar losses over high-dimensional inputs.
    • Lilian Weng — "A Peek into the Backpropagation Algorithm" (lil'log, 2017) — https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2017-06-21-overview/ — a detailed walkthrough of the matrix calculus underlying the backward pass, with worked examples for convolutional and recurrent layers that extend the two-layer MLP derivation here.
    • Hinton (2022) — "The Forward-Forward Algorithm: Some Preliminary Investigations" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.13345 — proposes replacing the backward pass with two contrastive forward passes; the paper's failure analysis on deep networks is the clearest statement of what backpropagation does that local learning rules cannot yet replicate.
    • Distill.pub — "Calculus on Computational Graphs: Backpropagation" (Olah, 2015) — https://distill.pub/2015/calculus-on-computational-graphs/ — an interactive visual treatment of the computation graph perspective; the node-elimination view of backpropagation complements the layer-by-layer derivation in the Mathematical foundations section above.
    • Chen et al. (2016) — "Training Deep Nets with Sublinear Memory Cost" — https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.06174 — the paper that introduced gradient checkpointing as a systematic technique; the memory-compute tradeoff analysis explains why modern training frameworks default to checkpointing every transformer block.
