    Transformer Architecture¶

    TL;DR: The transformer replaced sequential recurrence with parallel token-to-token routing via self-attention — now the universal backbone for language, vision, and multimodal AI, and the subject of active research into whether its quadratic inference cost can be eliminated without sacrificing its expressive power.

    What it is¶

    Imagine translating a novel where the only tool available is a single sticky note passed from word to word. Each word reads the note, scribbles an update, and hands it forward. By chapter three, the note is a smeared palimpsest — the opening sentence has been overwritten so many times it is unrecognizable. This is the fundamental bottleneck of LSTMs and GRUs: a fixed-size hidden state that must compress the entire history of a sequence into a single vector, then pass it forward one step at a time. The further apart two relevant tokens are, the more that signal degrades. The transformer shattered this constraint by giving every token a direct, high-bandwidth connection to every other token simultaneously — not a telephone game, but a conference call where every participant can hear every other participant at once, regardless of how far apart they are seated.

    The mechanism that enables this is self-attention. For each token, the model computes three vectors — a query, a key, and a value — by projecting the token's embedding through learned weight matrices. The query of one token is compared against the keys of all other tokens to produce attention weights, which then determine how much of each token's value vector to mix into the output. Because this comparison happens in parallel across all token pairs, the entire operation reduces to a matrix multiplication — fast on modern accelerators, and free of the sequential dependency that made RNNs slow to train. Stack this attention mechanism with a position-wise feedforward network, wrap both in residual connections and layer normalization, and repeat the block N times: that is a transformer.

    What makes the transformer more than an engineering convenience is that it reframes sequence modeling as a spatial routing problem. Rather than asking "what happened before this token?", it asks "which tokens are most relevant to this token, and by how much?" The feedforward sublayer then acts as a per-token memory lookup — research by Geva et al. (2021) showed that FFN layers function as key-value stores, retrieving factual associations independently of position. Together, the attention and feedforward sublayers form a residual stream: a shared information highway that each layer reads from and writes to, accumulating increasingly abstract representations as depth increases.

    Why it matters at the frontier¶

    The transformer's dominance is not accidental — it is a consequence of hardware alignment. Self-attention and feedforward projections are dense matrix multiplications, which map almost perfectly onto the parallelism of modern GPUs and TPUs. This alignment allowed transformers to scale in ways that RNNs structurally could not: more data, more parameters, more compute, reliably producing better models. The scaling laws documented by Kaplan et al. (2020) and later refined by Hoffmann et al. (2022) are transformer-specific observations, and they have driven every major lab's infrastructure investment for the past five years. GPT-4, Gemini Ultra, Claude 3, and Llama 3 are all transformer-based, and the engineering ecosystems — vLLM, FlashAttention, tensor parallelism — are built around the transformer's computational profile.

    The frontier tension is now the quadratic cost of attention. As context windows grow from 4K to 128K to 1M tokens, the memory and compute required for full self-attention scales as \(O(n^2)\) in sequence length, making long-context inference increasingly expensive. This has made the transformer's own success a pressure point: the architecture that enabled scaling is now the bottleneck to further scaling. The response from frontier labs has been a wave of hybrid architectures — Mamba, RWKV, RetNet, and most recently NVIDIA's Nemotron-H — that interleave attention layers with linear-time state-space or recurrent layers, preserving the reasoning capability of attention while reducing its cost at long contexts. Whether these hybrids can match pure transformers on all tasks, or whether some capability is intrinsically tied to full quadratic attention, is the central open question driving architecture research in 2025.

    Core concepts¶

    • Token embedding — a learned mapping from discrete vocabulary indices to dense vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^d\), where \(d\) is the model dimension; the input representation before any attention is applied.
    • Query, Key, Value (QKV) projections — three learned linear transformations applied to each token's embedding to produce the vectors used in attention score computation.
    • Scaled dot-product attention — the core operation: dot-product similarity between queries and keys, scaled by \(\sqrt{d_k}\), softmaxed to produce weights, then applied to values.
    • Multi-head attention — running \(h\) independent attention operations in parallel on lower-dimensional projections, then concatenating and projecting the results; allows the model to attend to different aspects of the sequence simultaneously.
    • Positional encoding — information injected into token embeddings (or attention scores) to give the model awareness of token order, since self-attention is permutation-invariant by default.
    • Residual stream — the shared vector that flows through the network; each sublayer (attention, FFN) reads from it and adds its output back to it, enabling gradient flow and interpretable layer-wise decomposition.
    • Feedforward sublayer — a two-layer MLP applied independently to each token position after attention; functions as a position-wise memory lookup with a typical expansion ratio of 4×.
    • Causal masking — in decoder-only models, a mask applied to attention weights that prevents each token from attending to future tokens, enforcing autoregressive generation.

    Mathematical foundations¶

    The core attention operation for a single head is:

    \[ \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V \]

    where \(Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_k}\) is the matrix of query vectors for all \(n\) tokens, \(K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_k}\) is the matrix of key vectors, \(V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_v}\) is the matrix of value vectors, and \(d_k\) is the dimension of the key vectors used for scaling. The scaling by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) prevents the dot products from growing large in magnitude as \(d_k\) increases, which would push the softmax into regions of near-zero gradient.

    Multi-head attention concatenates \(h\) such operations run on separate linear projections:

    \[ \text{MultiHead}(X) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \ldots, \text{head}_h)\,W^O \]
    \[ \text{head}_i = \text{Attention}(XW_i^Q,\; XW_i^K,\; XW_i^V) \]

    where \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) is the input sequence, \(W_i^Q, W_i^K \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_k}\) and \(W_i^V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_v}\) are the per-head projection matrices, and \(W^O \in \mathbb{R}^{hd_v \times d}\) is the output projection that maps the concatenated heads back to the model dimension. This equation says that each head learns a different subspace in which to measure token relevance, and the output projection mixes those subspace-specific signals back into a single representation.

    Shaw et al. (2018) extended the attention score to incorporate relative position information:

    \[ e_{ij} = \frac{(x_i W^Q)(x_j W^K + a_{ij}^K)^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} \]

    where \(x_i, x_j \in \mathbb{R}^d\) are the input embeddings at positions \(i\) and \(j\), \(W^Q\) and \(W^K\) are the query and key projection matrices, \(a_{ij}^K \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}\) is a learned relative position representation (a parameter vector) encoding the directed distance from position \(i\) to position \(j\), and \(d_k\) is the key dimension. This equation says that the relevance between two tokens depends not only on their content but on how far apart they are and in which direction — injecting sequence order dynamically into the attention score rather than adding a fixed offset to the input embedding.

    The full transformer block update for layer \(\ell\) is:

    [ x' = x + \text{MultiHead}(\text{LN}(x)) ] [ x^{\ell+1} = x' + \text{FFN}(\text{LN}(x')) ]

    where \(x \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) is the residual stream entering the block, \(\text{LN}\) denotes layer normalization (pre-norm formulation), \(\text{MultiHead}\) is the multi-head attention sublayer, and \(\text{FFN}\) is the position-wise feedforward sublayer. The additive residual structure means each layer's output is a correction to the previous representation rather than a full rewrite, which stabilizes gradient flow and allows the residual stream to be interpreted as a shared information bus.

    Key algorithms / techniques¶

    • Scaled dot-product attention (Vaswani et al., 2017) — the foundational QKV mechanism; computes pairwise token relevance via dot products scaled by \(\sqrt{d_k}\), enabling parallel sequence processing.
    • Multi-head attention (Vaswani et al., 2017) — runs \(h\) attention heads in parallel on projected subspaces, then concatenates; allows simultaneous attention to syntactic, semantic, and positional patterns.
    • Relative position representations (Shaw et al., 2018) — replaces fixed sinusoidal position embeddings with learned per-distance vectors added directly to attention logits, making position encoding dynamic and generalizable to unseen lengths.
    • Rotary Position Embedding / RoPE (Su et al., 2021) — encodes position by rotating query and key vectors in complex space; preserves relative position information through the dot product and has become the default in most modern LLMs including Llama and Mistral.
    • FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022; v2 2023) — a hardware-aware exact attention algorithm that tiles the computation to avoid materializing the full \(n \times n\) attention matrix in HBM, reducing memory from \(O(n^2)\) to \(O(n)\) and achieving 2–4× wall-clock speedup.
    • Grouped Query Attention / GQA (Ainslie et al., 2023) — shares key and value heads across groups of query heads, reducing KV cache memory at inference by a factor of \(h/g\) where \(g\) is the number of groups; used in Llama 3 and Mistral.
    • Sliding window attention (Beltagy et al., 2020; Longformer) — restricts each token to attend only to a local window of size \(w\), reducing complexity to \(O(nw)\); used in combination with global attention tokens for long-document tasks.
    • Hybrid attention + SSM layers (Gu & Dao, 2023; NVIDIA, 2025) — interleaves full self-attention layers with Mamba state-space layers that process tokens in \(O(n)\) time, achieving near-transformer quality at a fraction of the inference cost for long sequences.

    Current SotA¶

    As of mid-2025, the strongest publicly benchmarked language models remain transformer-based or transformer-hybrid. Meta's Llama 3.1 405B achieves 88.6 on MMLU and 73.0 on HumanEval. Google's Gemini 1.5 Pro extends the context window to 1M tokens using a mixture of full and sliding-window attention layers, achieving state-of-the-art on long-document retrieval benchmarks. NVIDIA's Nemotron-H 56B, a hybrid model with roughly 90% Mamba layers and 10% attention layers, matches or exceeds comparably-sized pure-transformer models on standard benchmarks while reducing inference-time KV cache memory by over 40% at 128K context length — the most concrete demonstration to date that hybrid architectures can close the quality gap.

    What's happening now¶

    Research frontiers. The dominant research question is no longer how to make transformers better in isolation, but how to replace or augment attention with sub-quadratic alternatives without losing capability. Mamba 2 (Dao & Gu, 2024) unified state-space models with linear attention under a single structured state-space duality framework, providing theoretical grounding for why SSMs can approximate attention in many settings. RWKV-6 and RetNet have similarly shown that linear-recurrent architectures can match transformer perplexity on language modeling at moderate context lengths. The open question is whether any of these alternatives preserve the full in-context learning and multi-step reasoning capabilities that emerge in large transformers — capabilities that may depend on the quadratic all-pairs interaction that linear methods approximate away.

    Engineering and systems. The engineering response to the quadratic bottleneck has been aggressive. FlashAttention-3 (2024) exploits NVIDIA H100 tensor core asynchrony to achieve near-theoretical peak utilization on attention, reducing the practical cost of full attention at long contexts. Speculative decoding, now deployed in production at Google and Meta, uses a small draft model to propose token sequences that the large transformer verifies in parallel, achieving 2–3× throughput improvements without changing model quality. KV cache quantization (INT8 and INT4 keys/values) has become standard practice for long-context serving, reducing memory pressure at the cost of minor quality degradation that is acceptable in most production settings. The vLLM project's PagedAttention manages KV cache memory with OS-style virtual paging, enabling high-throughput batched inference that was previously impossible due to memory fragmentation.

    Open problems. The central unresolved question is precise and consequential: What is the mathematically optimal ratio and interleaving pattern of self-attention layers to state-space layers that preserves full Turing-complete reasoning while minimizing quadratic inference costs? Nemotron-H uses a roughly 10% attention / 90% Mamba ratio chosen empirically, but there is no theoretical framework for predicting which tasks require full attention and how many attention layers are sufficient. A related open problem is whether positional encoding is a fundamental limitation: current long-context models degrade significantly beyond their training context length, and it is unclear whether this is a property of attention itself or of the position encoding schemes layered on top of it. Finally, the mechanistic basis for in-context learning — the ability of large transformers to implement new algorithms from examples in the prompt without gradient updates — remains poorly understood, with competing theories (gradient descent in the forward pass, Bayesian inference, associative memory retrieval) each supported by partial evidence.

    Minimum Valuable Build¶

    What you're building: A single hybrid Mamba-Transformer block in PyTorch — one self-attention layer interleaved with one Mamba SSM layer — trained as a character-level language model on TinyShakespeare.

    Why this build: This is the architecture pattern driving frontier hybrid models like Nemotron-H. Building it from scratch makes the trade-off concrete: the attention layer handles long-range dependency; the Mamba layer handles local sequential structure in \(O(n)\) time. Seeing both in one forward pass is more instructive than reading about either in isolation.

    Stack: Python 3.10+, PyTorch 2.2+, mamba-ssm 1.2.0 (requires CUDA), einops 0.7.0. Runs on a free Colab T4 (16GB VRAM) or any RTX 3080/3090.

    Estimated time: 45–60 minutes end-to-end (15 min setup, 20 min training, rest for experimentation).

    1. Download TinyShakespeare and build the character tokenizer

url = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/karpathy/char-rnn/master/data/tinyshakespeare/input.txt"

    1. Define the hybrid block: one attention layer + one Mamba layer
    import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F
from einops import rearrange
from mamba_ssm import Mamba

device = torch.device("cuda" if torch.cuda.is_available() else "cpu")

class CausalSelfAttention(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_model, n_heads, block_size):
        super().__init__()
        assert d_model % n_heads == 0
        self.n_heads = n_heads
        self.d_head = d_model // n_heads
        self.qkv = nn.Linear(d_model, 3 * d_model, bias=False)
        self.proj = nn.Linear(d_model, d_model, bias=False)
        # Causal mask: lower-triangular
        self.register_buffer(
            "mask",
            torch.tril(torch.ones(block_size, block_size)).view(1, 1, block_size, block_size)
        )

    def forward(self, x):
        B, T, C = x.shape
        q, k, v = self.qkv(x).split(C, dim=2)
        # Reshape to (B, n_heads, T, d_head)
        q = rearrange(q, "b t (h d) -> b h t d", h=self.n_heads)
        k = rearrange(k, "b t (h d) -> b h t d", h=self.n_heads)
        v = rearrange(v, "b t (h d) -> b h t d", h=self.n_heads)
        # Scaled dot-product attention with causal mask
        scale = self.d_head ** -0.5
        att = (q @ k.transpose(-2, -1)) * scale
        att = att.masked_fill(self.mask[:, :, :T, :T] == 0, float("-inf"))
        att = F.softmax(att, dim=-1)
        y = att @ v
        y = rearrange(y, "b h t d -> b t (h d)")
        return self.proj(y)


class HybridBlock(nn.Module):
    """One attention sublayer + one Mamba sublayer, each with pre-norm and residual."""
    def __init__(self, d_model, n_heads, block_size, d_state=16, d_conv=4, expand=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.ln1 = nn.LayerNorm(d_model)
        self.attn = CausalSelfAttention(d_model, n_heads, block_size)
        self.ln2 = nn.LayerNorm(d_model)
        # Mamba SSM: processes the sequence in O(n) time
        self.mamba = Mamba(
            d_model=d_model,
            d_state=d_state,    # SSM state expansion factor
            d_conv=d_conv,      # local convolution width
            expand=expand,      # block expansion factor
        )

    def forward(self, x):
        x = x + self.attn(self.ln1(x))   # attention residual
        x = x + self.mamba(self.ln2(x))  # Mamba residual
        return x


class HybridLM(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, vocab_size, d_model, n_heads, block_size, n_layers):
        super().__init__()
        self.embed = nn.Embedding(vocab_size, d_model)
        self.pos_embed = nn.Embedding(block_size, d_model)
        self.blocks = nn.Sequential(*[
            HybridBlock(d_model, n_heads, block_size) for _ in range(n_layers)
        ])
        self.ln_f = nn.LayerNorm(d_model)
        self.head = nn.Linear(d_model, vocab_size, bias=False)
        self.block_size = block_size

    def forward(self, idx, targets=None):
        B, T = idx.shape
        assert T <= self.block_size
        tok = self.embed(idx)
        pos = self.pos_embed(torch.arange(T, device=idx.device))
        x = tok + pos
        x = self.blocks(x)
        x = self.ln_f(x)
        logits = self.head(x)
        loss = None
        if targets is not None:
            loss = F.cross_entropy(logits.view(-1, logits.size(-1)), targets.view(-1))
        return logits, loss
    1. Instantiate the model and verify parameter count
    BLOCK_SIZE = 256
D_MODEL    = 256
N_HEADS    = 8
N_LAYERS   = 4   # 4 hybrid blocks = 4 attention + 4 Mamba layers

model = HybridLM(
    vocab_size=vocab_size,
    d_model=D_MODEL,
    n_heads=N_HEADS,
    block_size=BLOCK_SIZE,
    n_layers=N_LAYERS,
).to(device)

n_params = sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters())
print(f"Parameters: {n_params/1e6:.2f}M")
# Expected: ~6–8M parameters

# Sanity check: one forward pass
dummy = torch.zeros(2, BLOCK_SIZE, dtype=torch.long, device=device)
logits, _ = model(dummy)
assert logits.shape == (2, BLOCK_SIZE, vocab_size), f"Unexpected shape: {logits.shape}"
print("Forward pass shape check passed.")
    1. Write the training loop
    def get_batch(split, batch_size=32):
    data = train_data if split == "train" else val_data
    ix = torch.randint(len(data) - BLOCK_SIZE, (batch_size,))
    x = torch.stack([data[i : i + BLOCK_SIZE] for i in ix]).to(device)
    y = torch.stack([data[i + 1 : i + BLOCK_SIZE + 1] for i in ix]).to(device)
    return x, y

optimizer = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4, weight_decay=0.1)
scheduler = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(optimizer, T_max=3000)

@torch.no_grad()
def estimate_loss(eval_iters=100):
    model.eval()
    out = {}
    for split in ["train", "val"]:
        losses = []
        for _ in range(eval_iters):
            xb, yb = get_batch(split)
            _, loss = model(xb, yb)
            losses.append(loss.item())
        out[split] = sum(losses) / len(losses)
    model.train()
    return out

model.train()
for step in range(3001):
    xb, yb = get_batch("train")
    _, loss = model(xb, yb)
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
    optimizer.step()
    scheduler.step()

    if step % 500 == 0:
        metrics = estimate_loss()
        print(f"Step {step:4d} | train loss {metrics['train']:.4f} | val loss {metrics['val']:.4f}")
    1. Generate text from the trained model
    @torch.no_grad()
def generate(model, prompt, max_new_tokens=200, temperature=0.8):
    model.eval()
    idx = torch.tensor(encode(prompt), dtype=torch.long, device=device).unsqueeze(0)
    for _ in range(max_new_tokens):
        idx_cond = idx[:, -BLOCK_SIZE:]
        logits, _ = model(idx_cond)
        logits = logits[:, -1, :] / temperature
        probs = F.softmax(logits, dim=-1)
        next_tok = torch.multinomial(probs, num_samples=1)
        idx = torch.cat([idx, next_tok], dim=1)
    return decode(idx[0].tolist())

print(generate(model, prompt="\n", max_new_tokens=300))

    Expected output¶

    Training for 3000 steps on a T4 GPU takes approximately 8–12 minutes. Expected loss trajectory:

    • Step 0: train loss ~4.17, val loss ~4.17 (near random for 65-class vocab: \(\ln 65 \approx 4.17\))
    • Step 500: train loss ~2.0–2.2, val loss ~2.1–2.3
    • Step 1500: train loss ~1.6–1.8, val loss ~1.7–1.9
    • Step 3000: train loss ~1.4–1.6, val loss ~1.5–1.7

    Generated text at step 3000 will be recognizably Shakespearean in structure — consistent meter, character-name prefixes, plausible dialogue — though not semantically coherent. The key observation to make: the Mamba layers handle local character n-gram patterns efficiently, while the attention layers handle longer-range structural patterns like matching an opening speech tag to its closing line.

    Common failure modes¶

    • ImportError: mamba_ssm not found → Mamba requires CUDA; run in Colab with GPU runtime enabled (Runtime → Change runtime type → T4 GPU). CPU-only environments will not work.
    • CUDA out of memory → Reduce batch_size from 32 to 16, or reduce BLOCK_SIZE from 256 to 128. The model itself is small; the batch is usually the culprit.
    • Loss stays at ~4.17 after 500 steps → Check that targets are shifted by one position (i.e., y = data[i+1:i+BLOCK_SIZE+1]); a common bug is passing x == y which makes the task trivial but produces no learning signal.
    • AssertionError on forward pass shape → Ensure d_model % n_heads == 0; with D_MODEL=256 and N_HEADS=8, each head has dimension 32, which is valid.
    • Mamba layer produces NaN after a few steps → Add gradient clipping (clip_grad_norm_ with max norm 1.0) before optimizer.step(); Mamba's selective scan can produce large gradients early in training.

    Further reading¶

    • Vaswani et al. (2017) — "Attention Is All You Need" — https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762 — the original paper; reading the architecture section alongside the annotated implementation is the most efficient path to understanding the full model.
    • Shaw et al. (2018) — "Self-Attention with Relative Position Representations" — https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.02155 — explains why absolute position embeddings are limiting and how relative position representations fix the problem; the conceptual ancestor of RoPE.
    • Dao et al. (2022) — "FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with IO-Awareness" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14135 — explains the hardware-level reason attention was slow and how tiling solves it; essential for anyone deploying transformers at scale.
    • Lilian Weng — "The Transformer Family Version 2.0" (lil'log, 2023) — https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2023-01-27-the-transformer-family-v2/ — a comprehensive survey of transformer variants through 2023, covering sparse attention, linear attention, and positional encoding schemes with consistent notation.
    • Elhage et al. (2021) — "A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits" — https://transformer-circuits.pub/2021/framework/index.html — introduces the residual stream perspective and shows how attention heads compose; the foundation of mechanistic interpretability research on transformers.
    • NVIDIA (2025) — "Nemotron-H: A Family of Accurate and Efficient Hybrid Mamba-Transformer Models" — available via the Hugging Face model card at https://huggingface.co/nvidia/Nemotron-H-56B-Base — the most detailed public analysis of how to optimally mix attention and SSM layers, with ablations on layer ratio, interleaving pattern, and task-specific performance.
