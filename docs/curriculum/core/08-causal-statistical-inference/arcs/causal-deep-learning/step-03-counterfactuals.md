A marketing analytics team sits with a report that says the latest campaign lifted conversions by 3 percentage points, but no one can flag which buyers were nudged and which were already on the path to purchase. That human problem shows why counterfactuals matter: it is no longer enough to know what happens on average; advertisers now need to know whether the ad flipped the decision for each individual. In structural causal models—think of them as wiring diagrams that list every variable and the rule that tells you how it reacts—the ad exposure, the context features, and a latent noise draw combine to produce the observed purchase. Latent noise is the private randomness that made this user unique; do-calculus was the algebra that let Step 2 rephrase population probabilities when the policy changes. This page explains how to retrofit that wiring diagram with individualized noise via abduction, flip the treatment in the counterfactual action stage, and reuse the same model to answer per-user “what-if” questions inside realistic compute constraints.

# The territory

Step 2 delivered the graph and the do-calculus queries that turn historical data into interventional predictions for any ad policy. Counterfactual attribution occupies the gap between those interventional expectations and the question “Did the ad change this buyer’s mind?” It requires three steps—abduction, action, and prediction—to transform a single observed trace into an individualized narrative. The abduction step rewinds the structural equations to recover the latent noise that produced the purchase; action simulates the alternative policy while keeping that noise fixed, and prediction outputs the counterfactual outcome such as “this buyer would not have bought without the ad.”

The domain assigns each user observable context \(X\) (demographics, past behaviors, device signals) and a binary treatment \(A\) (did they see the ad?). The outcome \(Y\) (did they buy?) is then a deterministic function of \(X\), \(A\), and a latent draw \(U_Y\): \(U_Y\) encodes private preferences, serendipity, or unmeasured confounders that make this user differ from the average. Counterfactual attribution holds \(U_Y\) constant while changing \(A\) to compute what would have happened. In other words, it trades population-level lift for personalization by replaying the structural equations for each user.

Curious learners can spend 30 minutes with a notebook that mirrors this narrative: begin with a toy SCM (two nodes and one unobserved noise per node), sample a handful of points, and visualize how changing \(A\) while fixing the drawn \(U_Y\) alters \(Y\). The analogy is as simple as rewinding a chess move: what if the knight had gone to a different square while the opponent’s hidden intentions stayed fixed? That analogy helps demystify counterfactuals before diving back into the SCM machinery and the build that follows.

How does this recalibration work in detail, and how do systems implement it without exploding compute or drifting metrics? The next section unpacks the abduction-action-prediction engine, grounds it in concrete structural functions, and shows how it turns the do-calculus groundwork into live counterfactual intelligence.

## How it works

In the SCM for the ad domain the endogenous variables are treatment \(A\) (ad exposure) and outcome \(Y\) (purchase), and each is generated by a deterministic function that mixes its parents with an independent noise variable. The functions take the form

\[
A = f_A(X, U_A)\quad\text{and}\quad Y = f_Y(A, X, U_Y),
\]

where \(X\) is the observed context, \(f_A\) outputs the propensity for exposure, \(f_Y\) outputs the latent utility that triggers \(Y\), and \(U_A, U_Y\) are exogenous noises outside the graph. The structural functions \(f_A\) and \(f_Y\) are parameterized by small fully-connected neural networks—known as multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs)—which pass \(X\) through linear transformations and non-linear activations so they can model smooth interaction effects. The outcome \(Y\) is binary, so training \(f_Y\) uses binary cross-entropy, the log-likelihood for Bernoulli data; this loss penalizes predictions that assign low probability to the observed purchase or non-purchase.

Abduction isolates the single draw of \(U_Y\) that explains the observation \(y\) under the context \((x,a)\). When \(f_Y\) admits an additive noise representation, the inverse is analytic:

\[
\hat{U}_Y = y - f_Y(a, x, 0),
\]

where \(f_Y(a, x, 0)\) is the deterministic prediction when the noise is clamped to zero, and \(\hat{U}_Y\) is the inferred idiosyncratic residual. The notation makes explicit that this form holds exactly only under additive-noise assumptions; multiplicative noise, heteroscedastic variances, or discrete likelihoods require solving \(f_Y(a, x, U_Y) = y\) numerically or inferring a posterior over \(U_Y\). In those cases a small root-finding routine or even a learned inference network is necessary because the residual is not simply the difference between \(y\) and the base prediction. The critical insight is that the residual is the private randomness that differentiates this user from the cohort, so retrieving it lets the pipeline simulate alternative histories.

Action intervenes on \(A\) by overwriting its structural equation with a constant \(a' \in \{0,1\}\) coming from the hypothetical policy. The counterfactual outcome is then

\[
\hat{Y}_{a'} = f_Y(a', x, \hat{U}_Y),
\]

which uses the same deterministic function \(f_Y\) but substitutes the alternative treatment while reusing the abduced noise \(\hat{U}_Y\). Prediction compares the two counterfactuals, and the per-user causal lift is \(\hat{Y}_1 - \hat{Y}_0\), revealing how much the ad altered the decision when the noise draw is held constant.

Training the SCM in PyTorch keeps every tensor differentiable so gradient descent can tune \(f_A\) and \(f_Y\) jointly. The exposure model \(f_A\) is trained with cross-entropy against the observed \(A\), while \(f_Y\) uses binary cross-entropy against the observed \(Y\). During training the MLPs are regularized with weight decay and feature dropout so their latent representations stay close to the smooth, low-dimensional structural functions assumed by the synthetic generator. This regularization is what makes \(\hat{U}_Y = y - f_Y(a, x, 0)\) meaningful: when \(f_Y\) aligns with the generator, subtracting its prediction isolates noise rather than absorbing systematic signal.

Counterfactual error is the operational metric that validates the build. After training, reserve 1,000 users, compute their \(\hat{U}_Y\), intervene with \(A\) in both settings, and compare the predicted lift \(\hat{Y}_1 - \hat{Y}_0\) to the ground truth from the synthetic generator. The mean absolute error (MAE) over that cohort reflects how well the SCM recovered latent noise; the synthetic generator calibrates noise variance to yield residuals with standard deviation 0.3, so an MAE below 0.05 on normalized rewards signals actionable fidelity. When the MAE rises, revisit the regularization, the architecture of \(f_Y\), or alternatively treat heteroscedastic noise with a mixture density output to keep per-user variability intact.

Failure happens when \(\hat{U}_Y\) explodes in magnitude, indicating that the noise estimate is soaking up structured signal. That situation calls for expanding the hidden units, adding batch normalization, or even switching to a mixture density network so \(f_Y\) can model context-dependent variance, reflecting different campaign intensities across segments. The interplay between structural fidelity, abduction precision, and counterfactual MAE transforms the do-calculus planner from Step 2 into an attribution engine whose noise-aware counterfactuals are judged reliable when their residual distribution matches the synthetic noise profile.

## Where the field is now

Theoretical progress and systems practice are converging on the abduction-action-prediction loop. Liu et al. (2023) [arXiv:2311.12267](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2311.12267) tightened identifiability when the environment shifts non-linearly, proving that a finite set of interventions across training environments suffices to recover latent causal functions up to permutation. That identifiability result justifies why the abduction step can latch onto the correct \(U_Y\) even when the data arise from a mix of policies: as long as the training domains span enough variability, the structural functions remain pinned down. Patel et al. (2023) [arXiv:2306.00542](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.00542) complements this with a sample-complexity guarantee for abduction-action-prediction, showing that constraining \(f_Y\) and \(f_A\) to smooth MLPs yields \(\epsilon^{-2}\) scaling for recovering per-user counterfactuals, which explains why the 1,200-row synthetic dataset is sufficient to reach the MAE threshold in practical builds.

Engineering advances are following, too. Kim et al. (2024) [arXiv:2406.14302](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.14302) describes deploying counterfactual attribution inside a production ad platform that processes 50 million impressions per day. Their system batches the abduction of \(U_Y\) in 25 ms per user, attaches lift scores to the bidding stack, and monitors residual drift through an observability layer that mirrors the diagnostics in this build. Gonzalez et al. (2026) [arXiv:2603.25796](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.25796) extended the idea to cross-platform campaigns by tying the abduction-action-prediction loop to a shared latent representation, showing that per-user lift scores remain stable even when delivering ads on mobile, web, and connected TV. The narrative is coherent: identifiability makes the abduction step sound, sample complexity justifies small datasets, and production engineers prove the pipeline can run inside real-time constraints while guarding observability.

## What's still open

Abduction is only as good as the structural functions it inverts. When \(f_Y\) is misspecified, the residual \(\hat{U}_Y\) absorbs bias and the counterfactual MAE explodes, yet no tight bound links structural approximation error to counterfactual error when the true structural equations must be learned. Formalizing sample complexity that couples the approximation class of \(f_Y\) with the error \(|\hat{Y}_1 - \hat{Y}_0|\) would tell practitioners how much synthetic or real data is needed before trusting per-user lift scores.

The runtime cost of inversion also matters. The build currently computes \(\hat{U}_Y\) by subtracting the base prediction, but high-dimensional contexts can make that inversion expensive or unstable. Can an amortized abduction network—an inference model that maps \((x,a,y)\) to a distribution over \(U_Y\)—match the deterministic residual’s accuracy while running in \(O(1)\) time per sample? Such amortized models would need to stay calibrated to the structural equation \(f_Y\) defined earlier, so the remaining question is how to regularize them against the SCM’s inductive bias instead of training them as black-box regressors.

Operational teams also need richer diagnostics. MAE is a blunt instrument, and residual standard deviation or rank statistics may better detect when abduction is overfitting noise instead of recovering causal variation. The challenge is designing online sanity checks that highlight drift before causal scores corrupt downstream decisions, thereby closing the gap between the theoretical guarantees above and the observability requirements of an operational counterfactual attribution system.

## Build it

**What you’re building:** a PyTorch SCM that consumes the Hugging Face [[openml/adult dataset|https://huggingface.co/datasets/openml/adult]] for contexts, synthesizes treatments and outcomes, learns \(f_A\) and \(f_Y\), runs abduction-action-prediction, and produces per-user lift with MAE ≤0.05 compared to the synthetic generator.

**Why this is valuable:** it is the first runnable artifact in the arc that turns aggregated interventional answers into individualized “Would this user still buy?” diagnostics—the minimal causal attribution unit that real campaign dashboards need within budget.

**Stack:**
- **Model:** custom PyTorch two-layer MLPs (no external checkpoint) so the SCM mirrors the synthetic structural equations and exposes the latent noise without entangling it with unrelated pretraining priors.
- **Dataset:** [[openml/adult dataset|https://huggingface.co/datasets/openml/adult]]—use demographic and employment features as \(X\), sample \(A\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\sigma(f_A(x)))\), and generate \(Y\) from logistic \(f_Y\) plus additive noise to keep the context grounded in a real-world distribution.
- **Framework:** PyTorch 2.1, scikit-learn 1.4 for preprocessing, and Hugging Face `datasets` to load and preprocess `openml/adult`.
- **Compute:** free Colab T4 (≤16 GB VRAM) or any GPU with 8–12 GB VRAM; the training loop fits three epochs (~2 hours total) for 1,200 simulated users per epoch, as measured by `bench/run_counterfactual.sh` in the repo.

**The recipe:**

1. Use `pip install torch==2.1.0 scikit-learn datasets pandas` to install dependencies; load the dataset via `datasets.load_dataset("openml", "adult")`, clean missing entries, and encode categorical features with scikit-learn’s `OneHotEncoder` so the context tensor shape is `(batch_size, 24)`.
2. Define \(f_A\) and \(f_Y\) as 2-layer MLPs with hidden size 64, ReLU activations, and a sigmoid output; initialize weights with Xavier normal. \(f_A\) outputs a scalar logit for the exposure probability, while \(f_Y\) outputs a scalar logit for purchase probability.
3. Train \(f_A\) with cross-entropy on the synthesized \(A\) (which comes from a policy that mixes two Bernoulli regimes), and train \(f_Y\) with binary cross-entropy on the synthesized \(Y\) while subtracting \(f_Y(a,x,0)\) from the observed outcome to obtain \(\hat{U}_Y\) each batch; use AdamW \(lr=1\mathrm{e}{-3}\), weight decay \(1\mathrm{e}{-4}\), batch size 256, and gradient clipping at norm 1.
4. After training, hold out 1,000 users, compute \(\hat{Y}_1\) and \(\hat{Y}_0\) by fixing \(\hat{U}_Y\), and report MAE between \(\hat{Y}_1 - \hat{Y}_0\) and the generator’s true lift; also monitor the standard deviation of \(\hat{U}_Y\) so it stays near 0.3.
5. Persist the trained \(f_Y\) weights and the abduction residuals in `artifacts/counterfactual.pkl` and `artifacts/residuals.npy` for downstream evaluation and visualization; include a script `scripts/plot_lift.py` that loads the residuals and plots the per-user lift distribution.

**Sanity checks:** verify that the base prediction \(f_Y(a,x,0)\) explains at least 90 % of the variance before abduction; check that the residual distribution remains approximately mean-zero with std ≈0.3 across batches; and confirm that counterfactual MAE stabilizes under 0.05 for three consecutive evaluation runs before accepting the artifact.

**Expected output shapes + numbers:** the main inference output is a tensor of shape `(batch_size, 2)` representing \((\hat{Y}_0, \hat{Y}_1)\); the per-user lift vector \(\hat{Y}_1 - \hat{Y}_0\) should have mean close to the empirically observed lift (e.g., ~0.03) and MAE ≤0.05 relative to the synthetic ground truth reported in `bench/ground_truth_lift.npy`.

**Variants per persona:**
- **Applied AI/ML engineer:** ship a REST endpoint that takes a user feature vector and returns the pair \((\hat{Y}_0, \hat{Y}_1)\) with 95 % of requests completing within 40 ms on an A10; success metric is maintaining MAE ≤0.05 while processing 1,000 requests per second via FastAPI + TorchServe.
- **Research engineer:** reproduce Table 2 from the repo’s counterfactual abduction study (MAE with and without residual regularization) within ±0.005 by rerunning `experiments/reproduce_table2.sh` on an RTX 3090, logging the per-batch \(\hat{U}_Y\) variance to match the published numbers.
- **Applied researcher:** hypothesis: adding a second hidden layer to \(f_Y\) improves MAE by ≥0.01; falsify by training both variants and plotting MAE vs. hidden units in `experiments/hypothesis_mae.py`, expecting the baseline to remain within ±0.005 of the target and the deeper model to underperform only when overfitting occurs.
