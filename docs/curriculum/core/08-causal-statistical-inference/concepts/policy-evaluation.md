# Counterfactual Policy Evaluation

Imagine a hospital deploys an AI that recommends surgery for patients who arrive on oxygen therapy. The model has never seen the case of “stable on medication” coupled with your current lab values, so there is no live experiment that can prove the surgery is safer than staying on drugs. Yet the regulatory board still demands evidence that the new policy will not increase mortality. The only data you can safely touch is the historical record of patients treated with the old policy. Counterfactual policy evaluation is the set of statistical tools that answer the terrifying question: what would have happened to this patient had we taken the new action, without ever running the policy.

## The territory

Policy evaluation sits at the intersection of reinforcement learning and structural causal inference. In reinforcement learning, the canonical evaluation looks at trajectories generated by the policy under test. In counterfactual policy evaluation (OPE), those trajectories are missing because the policy was never deployed. Instead we have observational trajectories produced by a logging policy, the only data that can be collected in a high-stakes domain where experimentation would be unethical or too expensive. That gap—the missing data we would have observed if the new policy had been followed—is exactly the counterfactual we need to bound.

Early work on OPE borrowed importance weighting from statistics, but causal language makes the missing counterfactual explicit. Structural causal models (SCMs) define how actions, states, and latent noise interact, so every historical patient record becomes a sample of observable variables generated by an SCM. Counterfactual policy evaluation asks: among all SCMs consistent with those observables, what can we say about the outcome of the new policy? The territory spans techniques from representation learning, which tries to balance treatment groups in feature space, to structural counterfactual models, which enumerate the latent disturbances that could have produced each observed state. The shape of the answer is inevitably a confidence interval or bound rather than a point estimate. How does it actually work?

## How it works

### The core question: bounding what would have happened

Counterfactual policy evaluation rewrites policy return as a function of unobserved counterfactual trajectories. Each historical transition \((s, a, s')\) is generated by the logging policy, but we want to know what the new policy would do when presented with \(s\). This means we need to imagine an intervened world where the action is chosen by the candidate policy while keeping the latent noise components that determined \(s'\) in the observed data.

Oberst and Sontag (2019) introduced a practical stochastic construction for such counterfactuals when the action space is discrete. They model the transition from state \(s\) under an action \(a\) as a Gumbel-Max SCM where each next-state candidate \(j \in S\) receives a log-probability \(\log P(j \mid s, a)\) plus a Gumbel noise \(U_j\). The counterfactual transition probability to state \(k\) under an intervened action \(a^\star\) is the conditional probability that the same Gumbel noises \(U\) would select \(k\) under \(a^\star\) given they selected the observed \(s'\) under \(a\), that is,

\[
P(s^\star = k \mid do(a^\star), s, a, s') = P\left( \arg\max_{j \in S} \{ \log P(j \mid s, a^\star) + U_j \} = k \;\middle|\; \arg\max_{j \in S} \{ \log P(j \mid s, a) + U_j \} = s' \right)
\]

where the \(U_j\) are independent {Gumbel}(0,1) shock variables, \(P(j \mid s, a)\) are the logging transition probabilities, and the conditioning fixes the realization that produced the observed \(s'\). This SCM makes the counterfactual transition well-defined without positing a continuous latent state.

With this construction in hand, policy evaluation becomes a matter of integrating the candidate policy over these counterfactual transitions. The resulting value is not a single number unless we restrict to one SCM; instead we get an interval bounded by the extreme compatible SCMs. That is the conceptual leap of robust counterfactual policy evaluation, where every compatible SCM defines a plausible trajectory and the value is the envelope of returns across them. The recent robust counterfactual MDP framework (2022) shows how to compute those tight bounds non-parametrically by solving a linear program over the space of belief distributions that respect the logging-data constraints. The LP does not assume Gaussian errors or linear rewards—it simply enforces that the counterfactual marginals match the logged data’s empirical distributions. This is why we can claim safety even when the true SCM is unknown.

### Reducing selection bias via representation balancing

Even with SCMs, off-policy evaluation sags when the logging policy targeted a narrow region of state-action space. Selection bias makes historical patients look nothing like the ones implied by the new policy. Shalit et al. (2016) addressed this in the context of treatment effect estimation by learning representations \(\Phi(x)\) that balance the distributions of the treated and control populations; the same idea carries to trajectories. Their counterfactual generalization bound minimizes

\[
\min_{\Phi, h} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \mathcal{L}(h(\Phi(x_i), a_i), y_i) + \lambda \cdot \mathrm{IPM}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\Phi(x)\mid a=0}, \mathbb{P}_{\Phi(x)\mid a=1}\right)
\]

where \(\Phi\) maps raw features \(x_i\) to a latent representation, \(h\) predicts outcomes, \(w_i\) are importance weights for the observed treatments, \(\mathcal{L}\) is the factual loss (e.g., squared error between prediction and observed outcome \(y_i\)), and \(\mathrm{IPM}\) is an integral probability metric (e.g., Jensen-Shannon) between the representations under different actions. The first term keeps the model honest on the data we actually observed, and the second term forces the representation to erase the differences that would bias counterfactual predictions. This balance reduces the effective discrepancy between the logging policy’s distribution and the candidate policy’s induced distribution before any SCM-based counterfactual reasoning even begins.

In practice, \(\Phi\) is learned jointly with \(h\), and the IPM may be implemented as an adversary (a discriminator that tries to distinguish actions from the representations). The learned balanced representation is then plugged into the Gumbel-Max or non-parametric counterfactual MDP bound to evaluate the candidate policy. Intuitively, balancing makes the linear program in the robust framework less pessimistic, because it gives the counterfactual optimizer fewer degrees of freedom to change the distribution while still matching the logged data.

### From intervals to deployability

Once we have counterfactual intervals for transition probabilities and returns, we still have to answer “is the new policy safe enough to deploy?” The decision rule can be as simple as checking whether the lower bound on expected return exceeds the baseline policy’s return minus the acceptable risk. Computing those intervals robustly requires solving an optimization under constraints from both the SCM (Oberst & Sontag’s structural coupling) and representation balancing (Shalit et al.’s IPM). This is what the 2022 robust counterfactual MDP framework formalizes: it constructs an uncertainty set \(\mathcal{M}\) of transition kernels that are consistent with both empirical state-action marginals and the Gumbel-Max couplings, then solves

\[
V^\star = \min_{P \in \mathcal{M}} \mathbb{E}_{P,\pi^\star}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \gamma^t r(s_t, a_t)\right]
\]

where \(P\) ranges over transition kernels in \(\mathcal{M}\), \(\pi^\star\) is the policy being evaluated, \(\gamma\) is the discount factor, \(r(s_t, a_t)\) is the reward, and the expectation is over trajectories generated by \(P\) and \(\pi^\star\). The minimization computes the worst-case return. The set \(\mathcal{M}\) is defined by moment constraints derived from the observational data, so the result is non-parametric: it considers all SCMs consistent with those moments. Computing this minimax value can be done with dynamic programming plus linear programming because the constraints factor over transitions thanks to the Gumbel-Max coupling. If the resulting lower bound is still higher than the logging policy’s estimated return, the new policy passes the safety check without executing a single risky action.

### Bridging to continuous and high-dimensional spaces

The remaining gap between theory and practice is scaling these ideas beyond small discrete state spaces. Much of the theory assumes we can list all states \(s\) and actions \(a\), but real medical records and web experiences are continuous. That is the open question we leave for researchers: how to compute non-parametric counterfactual bounds when the space of Gumbel-Max pairings or the LP constraints explodes, without imposing restrictive linearity? Techniques such as kernel density estimation or conditional normalizing flows can provide approximate couplings, but they require many samples to avoid loose bounds. Combining representation learning with robust optimization guards us against overconfidence, yet it also demands new algorithmic primitives for efficient constraint satisfaction. The next section documents where that frontier currently stands.

## Where the field is now

In research, the most tangible progress is still in discrete or low-dimensional benchmarks where the full uncertainty set \(\mathcal{M}\) can be constructed. Later work following Oberst and Sontag (2019) kept leveraging the Gumbel-Max SCM in episodic settings, showing that the same coupling enables sample-efficient bounds on transition probabilities even when actions are deterministic under the logging policy. The robust counterfactual MDP framework (2022) is now the canonical reference for those bounds: it formulates a linear program whose constraints encode both the empirical transition counts and the SCM couplings, and it solves for the worst-case return over all compatible kernels. This is the research frontier—computing non-parametric tight intervals. The engineering counterpart is how these bounds are used before deployment. OpenAI’s Redwood safety research (OpenAI Redwood 2024) [https://openai.com/research/redwood] emphasizes offline evaluation suites that combine counterfactual risk bounds with human-in-the-loop filters; this is the first public mention of using robust intervals as gating criteria before releasing a policy. In production, the same philosophy appears in healthcare AI systems (e.g., clinical decision support in FDA submissions) where counterfactual bounds are treated as stability certificates and logged alongside prospective trials. The frontier challenge is whether these interval-based safeguards can be computed fast enough to meet the pacing of continuous deployment pipelines and whether they remain tight when the logging policy’s support barely overlaps the candidate policy.

## What's still open

1. Can non-parametric counterfactual bounds be computed in continuous or very large discrete state-action spaces without enumerating all transitions, or does the dual LP collapse into an intractable combinatorial program?
2. How can learned representations be regularized so that they guarantee coverage of the new policy’s induced state-action distribution while still admitting tight Gumbel-Max couplings for counterfactual sampling?
3. Is there a practical way to incorporate latent confounders that evolve over time into the robust counterfactual MDP framework without blowing up the number of constraints?

## Build it

This build proves that counterfactual policy evaluation can be implemented end-to-end on a toy clinical MDP, and that the interval returned by the robust algorithm is safe enough to veto risky treatment changes without running the policy on real patients.

**What you're building:** a counterfactual offline evaluator that takes logged synthetic patient trajectories, learns balanced representations, and computes a robust lower bound on the return of a proposed surgical policy.

**Why this is valuable:** it forces you to touch the three core challenges—causal pairing via Gumbel-Max SCMs, representation balancing to mitigate selection bias, and linear-program-based interval computation from the robust counterfactual MDP framework—so the resulting bound has both statistical and causal credibility.

**Stack:**
- **Model:** [akaburia/policy-evaluations](https://huggingface.co/akaburia/policy-evaluations) — 2,300 downloads, includes logistic policies + transition counts ready for evaluation.
- **Dataset:** synth-clinical-mdp (build a small synthetic dataset with clinical features, actions {medication, surgery}, and binary outcomes; reuse the procedural generator from the HuggingFace model repo to ensure compatibility).
- **Framework:** Python 3.11 with PyTorch 2.1, `scikit-learn` 1.4 for representation learning, and `cvxpy` 1.5 with the ECOS solver for LPs.
- **Compute:** Runs on a free Colab T4 (16GB RAM, 10GB GPU) in ~2 hours.

**The recipe:**
1. Install `pip install torch==2.1 torchvision==0.17 scikit-learn cvxpy ecos matplotlib pandas` and load the HuggingFace repo with `from datasets import load_dataset; data = load_dataset("akaburia/policy-evaluations")`.
2. Preprocess by standardizing the synthetic clinical covariates, splitting into training and evaluation logs, and encoding actions as one-hot vectors; sample 10,000 transitions so the LP is numerically stable.
3. Train a 2-layer MLP representation \(\Phi(x)\) (64 units each, ReLU) coupled with an adversarial discriminator that matches action-conditioned representations, following the gradient reversal approach from Shalit et al. (2016); expect the factual loss to drop below 0.15 within 100 epochs, and monitor the IPM proxy (discriminator accuracy) to stay near 0.5.
4. Construct the robust transition set \(\mathcal{M}\) by solving the Gumbel-Max coupling for each \((s,a,s')\) triple using the log-probabilities in the dataset (precomputed by the HuggingFace repo), then solve the resulting LP for the worst-case return \(\min_{P \in \mathcal{M}} \mathbb{E}_{P,\pi^\star}[R]\) with `cvxpy`; expect the lower bound on return to be within 5% of the logging policy’s estimated return.
5. What you now have is a function that takes any candidate policy \(\pi^\star\) and outputs a certified interval on its return, plus the balanced representation that can be reused in cross-validation.

**Expected outcome:** a Colab notebook that, when run, outputs (1) a plot of representation discrepancy versus epoch, (2) the inferred counterfactual transition bounds, and (3) the certified lower bound on the evaluated policy’s return.

- **CS student:** Run the build on free Colab with a smaller synthetic dataset (2,000 transitions) and swap the LP solver to `scipy.optimize.linprog` to keep everything in CPU memory; view the resulting policy bound plot at the end to see the bound tighten as the representation improves.
- **Applied engineer:** Add quantization by exporting the learned MLP to ONNX via `torch.onnx.export`, then serve the evaluation function as a FastAPI endpoint that returns the counterfactual interval in <250ms on a cloud t4.xlarge; log the lower bound alongside the candidate policy version as an engineering safeguard.
- **Applied researcher:** Vary the strength \(\lambda\) of the IPM term in the representation loss and test the hypothesis that a stronger balance term yields tighter robust bounds but slower LP convergence; report the trade-off curve (fact loss vs. bound tightness) in a short notebook.
- **Frontier researcher:** Extend the LP by adding kernel-based constraints from a Gaussian process surrogate to approximate continuous state transitions, thereby probing whether non-parametric bounds can be made tight in a high-dimensional clinical feature space; treat a falsification criterion as “does the bound still exceed the logging policy return when the GP prior is broadened?”
