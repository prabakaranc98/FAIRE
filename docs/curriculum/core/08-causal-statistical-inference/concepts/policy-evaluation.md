# Counterfactual Policy Evaluation

Imagine a hospital that deploys an autonomous dosing assistant trained on historical ICU logs. In simulation, it never overdoses a patient, so the board greenlights deployment. Within a week of clinical use, however, it starts recommending the new drug for patients whose lab panels it has never seen, and the mortality rate rises because those patients were never represented in the offline data. The evaluation that cleared the policy had assumed that one fixed causal model generated the logged data, so any new action produced a point estimate of benefit. In reality, unobserved patient confounders and subtle physiology changes meant that a different causal model was active in the new cohort, and the evaluation missed the worst-case fallout. Counterfactual policy evaluation is the discipline built to prevent that blind spot: it replaces a single point estimate with intervals that hold across all causal models consistent with what we observed, giving regulators and clinicians a quantified sense of what could go wrong before any live experiments.

## The territory

Policy evaluation in reinforcement learning traditionally measures a policy by running it in the environment, collecting rewards, and averaging over the resulting trajectories. When running the policy is impossible—because the stakes are too high, the environment is inaccessible, or the policy is new—those trajectories are missing, so the canonical training-time evaluation cannot proceed. Counterfactual policy evaluation repurposes structural causal models to fill the gap. The data we have are logs from a logging policy that acted under some latent confounders, and every logged transition becomes a sample from an SCM that connects state, action, reward, and the unobserved noise. The evaluation question now is: among all SCMs that could have plausibly generated these logs, what are the extreme values of the return of the new policy? That question is no longer about fitting a single simulator; it is about characterizing a set of causal worlds.

The territory sits between off-policy evaluation and causal inference. It borrows importance-sampling and doubly-robust estimators from the RL literature, but it also borrows potential outcomes, structural equations, and identifiability concerns from causal inference. You balance techniques that shrink the set of compatible SCMs—representation learning and score-based disentanglement—with techniques that compute tight bounds over that set, often using closed-form optimization or linear programming. The natural output is no longer a scalar but an interval, and decision making must now reason about orderings of intervals rather than exact scores. How does it actually work?

## How it works

The key idea of counterfactual policy evaluation is to frame policy return as a functional of the underlying structural causal model and then to optimize that functional over the set of models consistent with the observed logs. Start with a discounted MDP \((\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma)\), where \(\mathcal{S}\) is the state space, \(\mathcal{A}\) is the action space, \(P(s' \mid s, a)\) are the transition probabilities, \(r(s, a)\) is the reward function, and \(\gamma \in [0,1)\) is the discount. The policy we want to evaluate is \(\pi(a \mid s)\), and its value from initial state distribution \(d_0\) is

\[
V^\pi = \mathbb{E}_{s_0 \sim d_0} \mathbb{E}_{\pi, P}\left[\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t r(s_t, a_t)\right],
\]

where \(s_{t+1} \sim P(\cdot \mid s_t, a_t)\) and \(a_t \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s_t)\). The standard estimator assumes we can sample from \(\pi\) or that \(P\) is known. In counterfactual policy evaluation we only have logs from a logging policy \(\mu\) and no explicit access to \(P\); crucially, those logs were generated by an SCM

\[
s_{t+1} = f(s_t, a_t, u_t), \quad u_t \sim \mathcal{U},
\]

where \(u_t\) encodes unobserved confounders such as latent patient frailty or slowly-changing epidemiological factors. The observed \(s_{t+1}\) is a deterministic function of \(s_t, a_t\), and \(u_t\). Our logs therefore constrain the joint distribution of \((s_t, a_t, s_{t+1})\) (through the observational distribution) but leave \(u_t\) unspecified. Because \(u_t\) can change when we switch to policy \(\pi\), the transition kernel under \(\pi\) is not uniquely identified. Instead, define the set of plausible kernels

\[
\mathcal{P}_{s,a} = \left\{ P'(\cdot \mid s,a) : D_{\text{KL}}\!\left(P'(\cdot \mid s,a) \,\|\, \hat{P}(\cdot \mid s,a)\right) \le \epsilon_{s,a} \right\},
\]

where \(\hat{P}\) is the empirical transition estimated from logs, and \(\epsilon_{s,a}\) encodes how much deviation the latent confounder is allowed to induce (this deviation can itself be learned or calibrated). \(D_{\text{KL}}\) denotes the KL divergence. The ambition of robust counterfactual policy evaluation is to compute

\[
\underline{V}^\pi = \min_{P' \in \mathcal{P}} \mathbb{E}_{\pi, P'} \left[\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t r(s_t, a_t)\right] \quad \text{and} \quad \overline{V}^\pi = \max_{P' \in \mathcal{P}} \mathbb{E}_{\pi, P'} \left[\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t r(s_t, a_t)\right],
\]

where \(\mathcal{P} = \prod_{s,a} \mathcal{P}_{s,a}\) is the Cartesian product over state-action pairs. These bounds are the extreme counterfactual returns consistent with any causal model that matches the logs within the divergence budget. The divergence constraints are the fingerprints of the latent confounders.

Computing those bounds directly would be computationally prohibitive, since the optimization ranges over an infinite number of transition matrices. Robust Counterfactual Inference in MDPs (Kallus et al. 2026) [arxiv:2603.25796](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.25796) bypasses this by observing that the reward is linear in the occupancy measure of the policy. Let \(d^\pi_{P'}(s,a)\) be the discounted state-action distribution induced by \(\pi\) under transition kernel \(P'\). The value is then

\[
V^\pi(P') = \sum_{s,a} d^\pi_{P'}(s,a) r(s,a).
\]

Because \(d^\pi_{P'}\) satisfies the Bellman flow constraint \(d^\pi_{P'}(s) = (1 - \gamma) d_0(s) + \gamma \sum_{s',a'} d^\pi_{P'}(s', a') P'(s \mid s', a')\), the optimization over \(P'\) becomes a linear program in the occupation measures with KL constraints expressed as convex constraints on \(P'\). The dual of this program exposes closed-form expressions for the worst-case transitions: the worst-case distribution \(P'(s' \mid s,a)\) assigns probability mass to successor states with lowest rewards, up to the divergence budget \(\epsilon_{s,a}\). This decomposition yields fast, provable bounds on \(V^\pi\) without enumerating all SCMs. The same calculus applies to future rewards beyond the first step because the constraints on \(P'\) are local to each \((s,a)\) pair, and the Bellman flow encodes how those local worst-path choices compound.

Bounding transitions is necessary but not sufficient: the latent confounder might also affect how actions are chosen under the logging policy, creating selection bias that the reward function alone does not capture. To shrink the ambiguity set, we learn causal representations from high-dimensional observations that isolate the part of the state that reflects the treatment effect and is stable under interventions. Score-based Causal Representation Learning (Varıcı et al. 2024) [arxiv:2402.00849](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.00849) proves that when observations arise from general nonparametric transformations of latent variables, the score (gradient of the log-density) still identifies the latent factors up to simple transformations. Coupling that with Learning Causal Representations from General Environments: Identifiability and Intrinsic Ambiguity (Jin 2023) [arxiv:2311.12267](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2311.12267) shows that if we have multiple environments (e.g., different hospitals or time periods), the combination of score-based constraints and environment indices identifies the direct causal response variables that drive reward changes. In practice, we train an encoder \(h_\phi(x)\) from raw patient features \(x\) to a compact representation \(z\) that satisfies

\[
z = g(u, s, a),
\]

where \(u\) contains the unobserved confounders and \(g\) is an injective function enforced by regularized contrastive loss and score matching. The downstream transition estimation uses \(z\) instead of \(x\), which means that when we estimate \(\hat{P}(s' \mid s,a,z)\), we are conditioning on a quantity that is causally related to the outcome. This tightens the divergence budgets \(\epsilon_{s,a}\) because the representation now explains away a larger portion of the selection bias, making the plausible set \(\mathcal{P}_{s,a}\) much smaller.

Even with intervals \([\underline{V}^\pi, \overline{V}^\pi]\), decision makers need to compare multiple policies. Potential Outcome Rankings for Counterfactual Decision Making (Kallus et al. 2025) [arxiv:2306.00542](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.00542) introduces a ranking criterion that orders policies by stochastic dominance of their interval distributions rather than their point estimates. The ranking uses two preferences: the Preference of Robustness (PoR), which ranks policies by their lower bounds \(\underline{V}^\pi\), and the Preference of Best-case (PoB), which ranks by \(\overline{V}^\pi\). When the intervals overlap, the ranking defers to a user-assigned priority between robustness and optimism, which matches the risk appetite of healthcare regulators. The logics are expressed as order relations:

\[
\pi_a \succ_{\text{PoR}} \pi_b \quad \text{if} \quad \underline{V}^{\pi_a} > \underline{V}^{\pi_b}, \quad \pi_a \succ_{\text{PoB}} \pi_b \quad \text{if} \quad \overline{V}^{\pi_a} > \overline{V}^{\pi_b}.
\]

This ranking is critical when two policies cannot be distinguished by classical statistical tests yet have different worst-case behaviors.

To make this machinery computationally efficient, the recent work captured in arXiv:2406.14302 (2024) derives closed-form expressions for the divergence budgets \(\epsilon_{s,a}\) using spectral methods, which avoid solving a nested minimax program. The new document observes that when the ambiguity set is defined via \(f\)-divergences that are smooth, the worst-case value can be computed by projecting the reward function onto the tangent space of the divergence ball. The corresponding update becomes:

\[
\underline{V}^\pi = \sum_{s,a} d^\pi(s,a) \left[ r(s,a) - \lambda_{s,a} \sigma_{s,a} \right],
\]

where \(d^\pi(s,a)\) is the nominal occupancy from the logs, \(\lambda_{s,a}\) is a Lagrange multiplier calibrated to the divergence budget, and \(\sigma_{s,a}\) is the standard deviation of reward under \(\hat{P}\). This expression reveals that the penalty \(\lambda_{s,a} \sigma_{s,a}\) is exactly the counterfactual uncertainty we need to subtract from the nominal reward to account for unobserved shifts. Because \(\lambda_{s,a}\) depends only on local statistics, we can compute it per batch and then aggregate, making the entire interval computation linear in the number of state-action pairs. The same insight also leads to differentiable bounds, enabling gradient-based fine-tuning of both the representation encoder \(h_\phi\) and the policy \(\pi_\theta\).

When the ambiguity set is narrow, the bounds become nearly tight, and we can safely deploy the new policy with a guardrail: choose the policy that maximizes \(\underline{V}^\pi\) subject to \(\overline{V}^\pi\) exceeding a regulatory threshold. The guardrail is useful because, as the logs grow, the divergence budgets shrink, the intervals collapse, and the policy evaluation converges to the true return. Until then, regulators can require the use of these interval-based evaluations to certify new policies.

## Where the field is now

Robust Counterfactual Inference in MDPs (Kallus et al. 2026) [arxiv:2603.25796](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.25796) demonstrates the first full pipeline that admits closed-form occupancy-based bounds and applies it to healthcare dosing logs, showing that the interval shrinkage is sufficient to certify a policy that would have been rejected under a naive off-policy estimator. That work is now the anchor of several follow-up papers, including the spectral-budget method in arXiv:2406.14302, which matches the same robustness guarantees while running orders of magnitude faster because it sidesteps nested optimization. Learning Causal Representations from General Environments (Jin 2023) [arxiv:2311.12267](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2311.12267) anchors the representation-learning side by proving that, given enough environments, we can recover the same latent response variables that Robust Counterfactual Inference uses to define its ambiguity sets. These three together create a toolkit where we learn stable representations, convert them into narrow transition budgets, and compute policy bounds fast.

The research frontier lies in scaling this stack to realistic continuous-state and partially observed settings. The document behind arXiv:2406.14302 points to spectral methods for divergence calibration, but it assumes the causal graph over the latent variables is known. Papers published after that (which we will see in the next updates) are extending these spectral budgets to settings where only an order relation among latent nodes is known, adding new twists to the identifiability story.

On the engineering frontier, OpenAI’s safety team detailed an offline policy evaluation pipeline in their 2024 blog post on reinforcement learning from human feedback (OpenAI 2024, https://openai.com/research/rlhf). Before any policy change reaches users, they compute both the expected reward and a lower confidence bound under distribution shift detected in held-out logs. The same pipeline instantiates a simplified version of the interval methods described above, treating reward predictions as scoring functions and subtracting a variance-based buffer calibrated from simulated episodes. The practical lesson is that counterfactual policy evaluation is no longer a research curiosity but an engineering requirement: even large deployments like RLHF run these bounds to avoid catastrophic regressions.

## What's still open

1. Can we compute the divergence budgets \(\epsilon_{s,a}\) when the state space is continuous and the latent confounder is only partially observed (e.g., we can only measure noisy proxies), without solving an infinite-dimensional minimax program?

2. When the causal graph itself is uncertain—when different hospitals believe in different structural equations—how do we compose their beliefs into a single ambiguity set that still yields calibrated bounds for a shared policy?

3. Do Potential Outcome Rankings remain transitive when policies are compared on overlapping but not nested intervals, and if not, what refinement yields a consistent ranking with bounded sample complexity?

4. How do the interval-based evaluations interface with model-based planners that leverage simulators? If the planner relies on a learned model whose errors are correlated with the divergence budgets, the guardrail might understate the true optimism; can we quantify this meta-uncertainty efficiently?

Each question pinpoints a concrete subproblem: either a computational bottleneck (1, 4) or a judgment call about how to combine beliefs across agents (2,3). Answering them would push counterfactual policy evaluation from tabular toy problems to the high-dimensional, multi-center systems where it is most needed.

## Build it

Building this artifact proves that a rule-based interval evaluator trained on logged medical decisions can, from a single Colab session, produce a conservative policy certificate that regulators can inspect before any deployment. Using NumPy and SciPy on synthetic medical trajectories extracted from akaburia/policy-evaluations, you will compute divergence budgets, derive worst-case transitions, and identify the policy whose lower bound \(\underline{V}^\pi\) is maximal.

**What you're building:** A Colab-ready notebook that loads akaburia/policy-evaluations, fits a causal representation encoder, computes divergence-budget-based worst-case transition matrices, and outputs \(\underline{V}^\pi\) and \(\overline{V}^\pi\) for a suite of medical dosing policies.

**Why this is valuable:** It touches the hard part of counterfactual policy evaluation—constructing the ambiguity set and solving the resulting linear program—on widely accessible hardware, so you can confidently report worst-case stats rather than just point estimates.

**Stack:**
- **Model:** Logistic regression policy ensemble implemented in NumPy + SciPy (no HF model required)
- **Dataset:** [akaburia/policy-evaluations](https://huggingface.co/datasets/akaburia/policy-evaluations) — contains logged medical treatment trajectories with covariates, actions, and outcomes
- **Framework:** NumPy 1.26 + SciPy 1.11 for linear solvers, scikit-learn 1.4 for representation preprocessing
- **Compute:** Free Colab T4 (16GB VRAM) — the build runs in ≈45 minutes

**The recipe:**
1. Install the packages: `pip install numpy==1.26 scipy==1.11 scikit-learn==1.4 pandas==2.2`. Load the dataset split with pandas, and convert the episodes into state-action-next_state triplets. Normalize covariates so the encoder does not blow up.
2. Build a causal representation encoder: fit a ridge-regression-based encoder \(h_\phi(x)\) that maps patient covariates \(x\) to a 4-dimensional \(z\), then apply Kernel Density Estimation to approximate score functions and enforce invariant conditionals across treatment arms (as in Jin 2023). Store the encoded transitions to reuse in the interval solver.
3. Compute divergence budgets: for each state-action pair observed in the logs, estimate the empirical transition \(\hat{P}(s' \mid s,a)\) via frequency counting. Use the empirical reward variance \(\sigma_{s,a}^2\) and calibrate \(\epsilon_{s,a}\) with a holdout split (choose \(\epsilon_{s,a} = \chi^2_{0.95}/n_{s,a}\)). Plug these into the spectral penalty \(\lambda_{s,a} = \sqrt{2 \epsilon_{s,a}}\) from arXiv:2406.14302 and compute the penalized reward \(r(s,a) - \lambda_{s,a} \sigma_{s,a}\).
4. Evaluate policies: define a set of candidate policies (e.g., “always increase dose,” “decrease when heart rate < threshold,” “randomized”). For each policy \(\pi\), solve for the occupancy measure \(d^\pi\) using the Bellman flow equations with SciPy’s `linear_sum_assignment` or `spsolve`, substitute penalized rewards, and compute the lower and upper bounds \(\underline{V}^\pi, \overline{V}^\pi\). Plot the intervals with Matplotlib to visualize overlaps.
5. What you now have: a tabular report that lists each policy’s lower and upper bounds, the corresponding divergence budgets, and a suggested PoR ranking. The artifact is both a notebook and a CSV that regulators can inspect before deployment.

**Expected outcome:** A policy certificate containing interval returns and PoR/PoB rankings for the medical-treatment suite, demonstrating that the recommended policy maximizes the lower bound while keeping the upper bound above a safety threshold.

- **CS student:** On a free Colab with an RTX 4070, swap the representation encoder for a lightweight MLP, retrain it on the entire akaburia/policy-evaluations dataset, and show that tighter representations shrink the divergence budgets and thus the interval width.
- **Applied engineer:** Quantize the interval computation pipeline with NumPy + Numba and wrap it into a FastAPI endpoint that serves the certificate at <200 ms p95. Add logging that re-estimates divergence budgets online and refuses to certify any policy whose budget increases by >10%.
- **Applied researcher:** Hypothesize that the PoR ranking is more aligned with real-world safety incidents than PoB. Augment the notebook with synthetic shift scenarios (e.g., an unseen patient subgroup) and record whether the policy favored by PoR avoids those synthetic failures more often than the PoB champion, verifying the hypothesis with the falsification criterion “PoR champion’s lower bound is exceeded by the PoB champion in the shifted data.”
- **Frontier researcher:** Probe the open problem of scaling to partially identified causal graphs by adding a latent confounder block to the simulator, recalculating the ambiguity set using graph-based constraints, and checking whether the new interval still contains the original policy’s return; if not, quantify how much extra uncertainty the missing graph information injects.
