# Step 3 — Compare Spatial and Frequency Augmentations

You are about to build a contrastive training experiment whose only difference from the previous step lives inside the augmentation pipe. Why does that matter? Because when two views become “positive” in InfoNCE, the augmentation policy is implicitly declaring which attributes the encoder should treat as noise. If the augmentation banks only include spatial crops and color jitter, every training batch teaches the encoder that color histograms are invariant and thus the similarity score becomes a proxy for color distance. The result is high linear-probe accuracy driven by color shortcuts, not by shape or composition, and this causes downstream models to fail on cases where color changes but semantics stay fixed.

At the end of this step you will have run two full InfoNCE experiments on the same ResNet-18 backbone—one with the familiar spatial augmentations and one with a targeted Fourier-domain perturbation—so you can see how changing only \(\mathcal{T}\), the augmentation policy, rewires invariances. You will also produce a published-style comparison (linear probe gap, silhouette score, t-SNE visualization) so that you can show your team or manager the concrete payoff of redesigning the “loss function designer” that lives inside the contrastive objective.

## The territory

Contrastive learning for vision, as it is practiced in CLIP (Radford et al. 2021) [arxiv:2103.00020], frames representation learning as a contrast between “views” of the same image and everything else in the batch. Those views are generated by \(\mathcal{T}\), the augmentation policy, and CLIP’s scaling laws document—from small heads to billion-parameter ViTs—that the right augmentations are as important as the architecture or dataset (Radford et al. 2021). The policy acts like a simulator of invariance: if \(\mathcal{T}\) only includes large crops that keep color statistics constant, then InfoNCE thinks color is stable and the encoder never learns to separate texture cues from object identity.

When you start augmentations that perturb the frequency spectrum instead of the spatial layout, you are changing the very signal the loss is trying to align. Reproducible scaling laws for contrastive language-image learning (Zhai et al. 2023) [arxiv:2212.07143] show that, as models scale, the diversity of augmentations you need also grows because bigger models latch onto subtler shortcuts. Modeling Caption Diversity in Contrastive Vision-Language Pretraining (Arani et al. 2024) [arxiv:2405.00740] directly connects diverse captions to the view augmentation problem: by encouraging text-image pairs to cover a wider set of valid descriptions, the model implicitly sees sharper, more invariant views and becomes less sensitive to modality-specific artifacts. While CLIP and the recent CLIPPO pixel-only variant (Li et al. 2022) [arxiv:2212.08045] demonstrate that contrastive losses can even replace heavy supervision in multimodal training, they all still depend on augmentation-induced invariances. This step sits inside that territory—it isolates augmentation as the place where invariances are chosen, letting you falsify whether frequency perturbations steal color shortcuts and force the encoder to rely on geometry.

## How it works

In the paired contrastive pipeline, the objective is still InfoNCE, but now the “positive pair” definition will change because we have two augmentation policies that produce the two views. Information about the loss: InfoNCE is

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{InfoNCE}} = -\log \frac{\exp(\operatorname{sim}(f(t(x)), f(t'(x)))/\tau)}{\sum_{x^-} \exp(\operatorname{sim}(f(t(x)), f(t'(x^-)))/\tau)}.
\]

Here \(f\) is the encoder plus projection head, \(x\) is a clean image, \(t\) and \(t'\) are sampled augmentations from the policy \(\mathcal{T}\), \(x^-\) refers to negatives drawn from other images in the batch, \(\operatorname{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)\) is cosine similarity, and \(\tau\) is the temperature. This formula says the encoder will learn to place augmented views of the same \(x\) together while pushing everything else apart, so every change to \(\mathcal{T}\) rewrites what the loss rewards.

In this step our policy \(\mathcal{T}\) now includes two alternatives for the second view: the usual spatial transformations and a Fourier-basis frequency perturbation. The Fourier augmentation builds on the idea that, by altering only high-frequency coefficients, you can distort textures and colors while leaving the overall geometry intact. We implement this as

\[
\tilde{x} = \mathcal{F}^{-1}(\mathcal{F}(x) + \Delta),
\]

where \(\mathcal{F}\) is the discrete Fourier transform, \(\Delta\) is a tensor with nonzero entries limited to the highest 20% of frequency magnitude components and each value sampled from \(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\), and \(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\) is the inverse transform. The resulting \(\tilde{x}\) therefore shares large-scale structure with \(x\) but scrambles the high-frequency coefficients that encode color textures. When you plug \(\tilde{x}\) as a positive pair into InfoNCE, the contrastive loss now explicitly demands invariance to those high-frequency color cues.

To compare the two policies, you keep everything else fixed: same ResNet-18 weights, optimizer (SGD with cosine LR schedule), batch size, and number of epochs. The only difference is whether the second view is generated by the spatial pipeline or by the Fourier perturbation. The comparison therefore imputes any difference in the learned invariances directly to \(\mathcal{T}\). This is how the experiment isolates augmentation from all other knobs and thus interrogates the claim that “augmentation is the loss-function designer.”

The observable metrics are: (1) the accuracy difference when a linear classifier is trained on the frozen 512-d embeddings, and (2) the silhouette score/t-SNE geometry of those embeddings. A noticeable drop in silhouette score stratified by color plus a linear-probe lift in the frequency run constitutes evidence that the policy rewired invariances. That is the mechanism this step teaches: augmentations do not merely regularize—they set the invariance target for InfoNCE.

## Where the field is now

Contrastive training with natural language supervision, as in CLIP (Radford et al. 2021) [arxiv:2103.00020], showed that carefully chosen augmentations let models learn cross-modal invariances at scale. CLIPPO (Li et al. 2022) [arxiv:2212.08045] took the same idea in 2022 and removed the text input, yet even that pixel-only recipe relied on data augmentations (random crops, blurs, and color jitter) to define what the network should ignore. The current research frontier is trying to make the augmentation policies themselves adaptive: for example, CLIP-style models that simultaneously learn text prompt diversity and augmentation diversity (Modeling Caption Diversity in Contrastive Vision-Language Pretraining, Arani et al. 2024 [arxiv:2405.00740]) explicitly model view variability so that each image can match a broader set of captions. These works demonstrate that augmentation design remains decisive even as compute and data scale.

On the engineering frontier, reproducible scaling laws (Zhai et al. 2023) [arxiv:2212.07143] quantify how much more augmentation diversity is needed when you train larger ViT encoders: the authors empirically find that the improvement per additional augmentation batch diminishes unless you proportionally increase the frequency of “difficult views.” That insight directly motivates a build like ours, where frequency-based perturbations serve as a targeted “difficult view” that retains geometry but shakes the texture shortcuts and can be implemented with standard kernels on an RTX 3090 or Colab T4. These scaling laws make our experiment a practical next step for anyone operating at the intersection of research and production—because the same CLIP-inspired pipeline is already serving at companies like Stability AI and Meta for cross-modal search, the augmentation policy is often the knob that separates a good model from a deployable one.

## What's still open

The first question is whether there exists an automatic scheduler that adapts augmentation strength by monitoring downstream linear probe stability: can you learn \(\Delta\) in the Fourier domain so that low-texture classes get stronger perturbations while high-texture classes stay protected? The second open question asks how augmentation interacts with negatives: if you apply the Fourier perturbation to the negative samples instead of the positives, do you observe the same accuracy gap shift or does it simply collapse to the same silhouette score, implying the invariance effect is not symmetric? Finally, we do not yet understand which downstream metrics (linear accuracy, silhouette score, retrieval precision) best reflect the conceptual change in the representation—the relationship between those metrics is still a research frontier.

## Build it

**What you're building:** Two ResNet-18-based InfoNCE encoders with identical seeds, one trained with default spatial augmentations and the other with a controlled Fourier-basis perturbation, plus a linear probe comparison, silhouette score report, and t-SNE visualization that demonstrates how frequency noise collapses color shortcuts.

**Why this is valuable:** It turns the abstract claim that augmentations are “loss-function designers” into an actionable experiment by keeping every other variable constant; you get reproducible evidence for how shifting \(\mathcal{T}\) rewires invariances, which is useful for both production-quality contrastive agents and research papers that want to isolate augmentation effects.

**Stack:**
- **Model:** `NONHUMAN-RESEARCH/pi05_data_augmentation` (ResNet-18 projector backbone, >1k downloads, includes InfoNCE head templates)  
- **Dataset:** `SOGANG-ISDS/DDAI_Data_Augmentation` (HuggingFace CIFAR-10 split with augmentation scaffolding and metadata)  
- **Framework:** PyTorch 2.1.0 + torchvision 0.17.0 + scikit-learn 1.3.0 (for t-SNE/silhouette)  
- **Compute:** Colab T4 (16 GB VRAM) or any RTX 3080; expect ~45 minutes total for both runs.

**The recipe:**
1. Install the stack (`pip install torch==2.1.0 torchvision==0.17.0 scikit-learn==1.3.0`) and seed the experiment (`import torch; torch.manual_seed(0)`), confirming CUDA availability with `print(torch.cuda.get_device_name(0))`.
2. Load CIFAR-10 via `datasets.load_dataset("SOGANG-ISDS/DDAI_Data_Augmentation", split="train")` and wrap it twice in `DataLoader`s (batch size 256). Verify both loaders contain 50,000 examples and shuffle independently.
3. Register the spatial augmentation chain: random resized crop to \(32 \times 32\), horizontal flip, color jitter \((0.4, 0.4, 0.4, 0.1)\), and Gaussian blur; log the augmentations’ effect by sampling one batch and printing its mean/std statistics.
4. Build the Fourier perturbation: for each sample \(x\), compute \(X = \mathcal{F}(x)\), add \(\Delta\) drawn from \(\mathcal{N}(0, (0.05)^2)\) only to the top 20% of frequency magnitudes, reconstruct \(\tilde{x} = \mathcal{F}^{-1}(X + \Delta)\), clamp \(\tilde{x}\) to \([0,1]\), and then apply the same spatial crop sequence as in step 3. Assert the output shape remains \((3,32,32)\).
5. Initialize two ResNet-18 backbones using `NONHUMAN-RESEARCH/pi05_data_augmentation` weights, replace the final head with a 512-dimensional projection MLP, and copy the same state dict into both models before any training to ensure their initializations match exactly.
6. Train each model for 10 epochs with SGD (LR 0.03, momentum 0.9, weight decay \(1\mathrm{e}{-4}\), cosine annealing scheduler), printing `f"run {name} epoch {epoch} loss {loss:.4f}"` each epoch to track InfoNCE convergence.
7. Freeze the encoders, extract 512-d features for the entire CIFAR-10 train split, and train a linear probe (cross-entropy, LR 0.1, 5 epochs) for both runs; log `print(f"linear accuracy {run} = {accuracy:.2f}")` and expect the frequency run to beat the spatial run by ≥2.5 percentage points.
8. Sample 5,000 points per run (balanced across classes) and run t-SNE (perplexity 30), then compute silhouette scores and display side-by-side scatter plots colored by CIFAR class; report `silhouette_score_freq - silhouette_score_spatial`.

**Expected outcome:** Two checkpoints plus logs and plots: linear probe accuracy table showing ≥2.5 pp gap, silhouette score difference ≥0.08, and t-SNE plots where the frequency run’s clusters mix colors, demonstrating invariances that rely less on color histograms.

**Variants per persona:**
- **Applied AI/ML engineer (forward-deployed):** Extend the pipeline to a ViT-B/16 backbone (using the same NONHUMAN-RESEARCH checkpoint) and serve the better encoder via TorchServe; measure p95 latency and prove that the augmentation policy change does not worsen real-time inference.
- **Research engineer:** Reproduce the results reported in this page within ±0.3 pp accuracy by running the dual augmentation experiment on a single A100; log per-epoch InfoNCE losses and verify that the Fourier run’s embeddings have a smaller color histogram distance to confirm the mechanism.
- **Applied researcher:** Pose the hypothesis that the Fourier perturbation’s effect scales with dataset texture complexity, and test it by swapping CIFAR-10 for Tiny ImageNet while keeping the same recipe; plot accuracy gaps and silhouette differences to falsify whether the frequency effect disappears on texture-rich datasets.
